# 6. The don't-do list

This section collects things that "look cheap and effective." Open the original studies and their benefit runs near zero, at zero, or negative. Order follows two things: how much people spend on them, and how hard the supporting evidence is. The earlier, the sooner to stop.

Entries in this section are grouped by theme, with item numbers in parentheses.

**Supplements and health products**: multivitamins (item 1), ordinary fish oil (item 2), vitamin D supplementation (item 3), antioxidant supplements for cancer prevention (item 4), chondroitin for knee arthritis (item 5), vitamin C against colds (item 6), big spending on health products (item 10), long-term probiotics (item 11), "detox" and colon-cleanse products (item 13), mega-dosing calcium after a fracture (item 30).

**Checkups and small findings**: whole-body PET-CT and tumor-marker panels (item 7), generic checkup packages (item 18), high uric acid without gout attacks — don't medicate (item 19), gallstones that never hurt — don't remove (item 20), don't quit calcium to prevent kidney stones (item 21).

**Wellness lore**: fitness trackers for weight loss (item 8), organic food (item 9), cold showers for immunity (item 12), don't force yourself to drink water (item 14), blue-light glasses (item 16), blood donation as health practice (item 17), breakfast or intermittent fasting for weight control (item 26), lifting posture to prevent back pain (item 29).

**Buying luck and mood**: fortune-telling and horoscopes (item 15), crystals and pixiu charms (item 22), shopping to feel better (item 23), spending more to "move up a notch" (item 24).

**Willpower and sex**: willpower as a muscle (item 25), masturbation harming the body (item 27), conversion therapy (item 28).

**AI answers**: judging your own illness via AI chatbots (item 31).

### 1. Don't take multivitamins for longevity or cardiovascular protection
<!-- costs: money=none time=low will=no benefit=medium metric=money -->
- Cost: About 0.5–2 yuan a day, 200–700 a year. No time cost — one pill. Long use breeds a feeling that skipping a day means neglecting something.
- In plain terms: 14,000-odd male physicians took multivitamins for eleven years. Major heart and vascular events and deaths — the same as non-takers. Pooling 9 trials, 50,000-odd people: no effect on death either. Not buying saves 200–700 yuan a year. People with a confirmed deficiency and pregnant women taking folic acid aren't covered here.
- Benefit: The PHS II trial enrolled 14,641 male physicians, followed 11.2 years (median). Takers and non-takers had identical rates of major cardiovascular events. Major cardiovascular events HR 1.01 (HR is hazard ratio; 1 means identical). The 95% CI 0.91–1.10 is that number's confidence range. Death likewise: all-cause HR 0.94 (0.88–1.02). The US body that grades preventive services, USPSTF, pooled 9 randomized trials, 51,550 people in 2022: no association between multivitamins and total mortality. Rated I — evidence insufficient to say. It looks like a deal because "one pill fills every gap" sounds tidy and costs pennies a day
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Sesso HD et al. (2012). Multivitamins in the prevention of cardiovascular disease in men: the Physicians' Health Study II randomized controlled trial. JAMA. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2012.14805>；US Preventive Services Task Force (2022). Vitamin, Mineral, and Multivitamin Supplementation to Prevent Cardiovascular Disease and Cancer. JAMA. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2022.8970>；Gaziano JM et al. (2012). Multivitamins in the Prevention of Cancer in Men. JAMA. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2012.14641> (the cancer result in the note)
- Note: Contested: the same physicians were also followed for cancer. Takers had about 8% fewer cancers (0.86–0.998, P=0.04, barely clearing the "not chance" bar) and about 12% fewer cancer deaths (0.77–1.01) — a range crossing 1, so possibly chance. Small benefit, seen only in male physicians. Those with confirmed deficiency, pregnant women (folic acid), and the genuinely imbalanced diet follow doctor's orders — not this entry

### 2. Don't take ordinary fish oil to prevent cardiovascular disease
<!-- costs: money=none time=low will=no benefit=medium metric=money -->
- Cost: About 1–3 yuan a day, 400–1,000 a year. No time cost.
- In plain terms: Two big trials took 26,000 people and 15,000 people with diabetes. A daily gram of fish oil changed nothing in major cardiovascular events or deaths. "Fish is good for you" isn't "fish-oil capsules are good for you." Not buying saves 400–1,000 yuan a year.
- Benefit: The VITAL trial enrolled 25,871 people on 1 g daily, followed 5.3 years (median). Major cardiovascular events and death showed no difference. Major cardiovascular events HR 0.92 (HR is hazard ratio; 1 means identical); the 95% CI 0.80–1.06 is its confidence range, crossing 1 — no difference. All-cause death HR 1.02 (0.90–1.15). The ASCEND trial took 15,480 people with diabetes, also 1 g daily, average 7.4 years, likewise nothing: serious vascular events RR 0.97 (0.87–1.08); all-cause death RR 0.95 (0.86–1.05). It looks like a deal because "fish is good" got carried straight to "fish-oil capsules are good"
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Manson JE et al. (2019). Marine n-3 Fatty Acids and Prevention of Cardiovascular Disease and Cancer. NEJM. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1811403>；ASCEND Study Collaborative Group (2018). Effects of n-3 Fatty Acid Supplements in Diabetes Mellitus. NEJM. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1804989>；Bhatt DL et al. (2019). Cardiovascular Risk Reduction with Icosapent Ethyl for Hypertriglyceridemia. New England Journal of Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1812792> (the high-purity prescription trial in the note)
- Note: Contested: one trial worked. REDUCE-IT took 8,179 people with existing cardiovascular disease or diabetes, all on statins, triglycerides still high (135–499 mg/dL). On 4 g daily of prescription pure EPA, events ran 17.2% vs 22.0% — risk down about a quarter (HR 0.75, 0.68–0.83). But that's a high-dose prescription for high-risk patients, not a health-store fish oil for ordinary people. Eating fish itself isn't covered here either

### 3. Don't supplement vitamin D in people who aren't deficient, for longevity
<!-- costs: money=none time=low will=no benefit=medium metric=money -->
- Cost: About 0.2–0.5 yuan a day, 100–200 a year. The money is trivial; the real cost is believing you've done prevention.
- In plain terms: People not deficient, supplementing 5 years: cancers, cardiovascular disease, and deaths all matched the non-supplementers. The confirmed-deficient, people with osteoporosis, and those with little sun exposure aren't covered — they supplement per doctor's orders.
- Benefit: VITAL took 25,871 people on 2,000 IU daily, followed 5.3 years (median). Cancer, cardiovascular events, and death: no differences. Metastatic-cancer HR 0.96 (HR is hazard ratio; 1 means identical; 95% CI 0.88–1.06 the confidence range). Major cardiovascular events HR 0.97 (0.85–1.12). All-cause death HR 0.99 (0.87–1.12). The D-Health trial took 21,315 Australians over 60 on 60,000 IU monthly, followed 5 years — also nothing: all-cause death HR 1.04 (0.93–1.18); deaths 5.3% vs 5.1%. It looks like a deal because observational studies found low vitamin D levels correlate with more deaths, misread as "supplementing fixes it"
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Manson JE et al. (2019). Vitamin D Supplements and Prevention of Cancer and Cardiovascular Disease. NEJM. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1809944>；Neale RE et al. (2022). The D-Health Trial: a randomised controlled trial of the effect of vitamin D on mortality. Lancet Diabetes Endocrinol. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S2213-8587(21)00345-4>
- Note: Contested: neither trial specifically recruited deficient people, so the conclusion covers only the not-deficient. The confirmed-deficient, osteoporosis patients, the sun-deprived, and infants supplement per doctor. Only total mortality, cancer, and cardiovascular outcomes were examined here

### 4. Don't take antioxidant supplements (β-carotene, vitamin E, vitamin A) to prevent cancer
<!-- costs: money=none time=low will=no benefit=medium metric=mortality -->
- Cost: About 0.5–2 yuan a day. For smokers, the real cost is lung cancer risk climbing.
- In plain terms: This one isn't merely useless. Pooling 78 trials, nearly 300,000 people: antioxidant-takers died slightly more. In two big trials enrolling only smokers, β-carotene raised lung cancer 18%–28% and death 8%–17%. Smokers and asbestos-exposed people especially should not take it.
- Benefit: Cochrane pooled 78 randomized trials, 296,707 people. Antioxidant takers: all-cause mortality RR 1.02 (RR is risk ratio; 1 means identical; 95% CI 0.98–1.05 the confidence range). Restricted to the better-designed, less-bias-prone trials: RR 1.04 (1.01–1.07), slightly higher. β-carotene alone RR 1.05 (1.01–1.09); vitamin E alone RR 1.03 (1.00–1.05). The ATBC trial took 29,133 male smokers on 20 mg daily: lung cancer up 18% (3%–36%), total mortality up 8% (1%–16%). CARET took 18,314 smokers or asbestos-exposed: lung cancer risk up about 28% (RR 1.28, 1.04–1.57), death up about 17% (all-cause RR 1.17, 1.03–1.33). It looks like a deal because "antioxidant = anti-aging, anti-aging = cancer prevention" — each link sounds like common sense
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Bjelakovic G et al. (2012). Antioxidant supplements for prevention of mortality in healthy participants and patients with various diseases. Cochrane Database Syst Rev. <https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD007176.pub2>；The Alpha-Tocopherol, Beta Carotene Cancer Prevention Study Group (1994). The effect of vitamin E and beta carotene on the incidence of lung cancer and other cancers in male smokers. NEJM. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJM199404143301501>；Omenn GS et al. (1996). Effects of a combination of beta carotene and vitamin A on lung cancer and cardiovascular disease. NEJM. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJM199605023341802>
- Note: The US grading body USPSTF gave β-carotene and vitamin E a D in 2022 — recommend against. Smokers and the asbestos-exposed have already been harmed. Antioxidants naturally present in vegetables and fruit aren't covered — eat those

### 5. Don't count on glucosamine/chondroitin to treat knee arthritis
<!-- costs: money=none time=low will=no benefit=medium metric=money -->
- Cost: About 2–5 yuan a day, 700–1,800 a year.
- In plain terms: Knee-arthritis patients taking a fake pill for 24 weeks: 60% still report less pain. Glucosamine or chondroitin adds only 4–5 percentage points over that — small enough to be chance. Seven hundred to eighteen hundred a year buys essentially that 60% placebo effect.
- Benefit: The GAIT trial took 1,583 knee-osteoarthritis patients for 24 weeks. On placebo, 60.1% reported pain relief. Glucosamine beat it by 3.9 points (P=0.30); chondroitin by 5.3 (P=0.17); both together by 6.5 (P=0.09) — all too small to rule out chance. The active comparator celecoxib beat placebo by 10.0 points (P=0.008). It looks like a deal because "raw material for cartilage" sounds on-target, and the placebo alone already satisfies 60%
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Clegg DO et al. (2006). Glucosamine, chondroitin sulfate, and the two in combination for painful knee osteoarthritis. NEJM. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa052771>
- Note: Contested: in the same trial, the severe-pain subgroup on both together showed 79.2% response versus 54.3% control (P=0.002) — but the authors flag it as post-hoc data-dredging: a lead, not a conclusion. What works for knee arthritis is weight loss and muscle training — Section 2

### 6. Don't rely on vitamin C to prevent colds
<!-- costs: money=none time=low will=no benefit=small metric=money -->
- Cost: Effervescent tablets run 1–2 yuan each, 300–700 a year.
- In plain terms: Daily vitamin C won't stop you catching colds, and starting after symptoms begin works erratically at best. Daily use shortens a cold by about 8% — half a day less misery; judge for yourself whether that's worth a few hundred a year. Marathon runners and soldiers marching in cold regions are the exception: their colds dropped by half.
- Benefit: Cochrane pooled 29 controlled trials, 11,306 people. Daily vitamin C in ordinary people barely changes cold incidence: RR 0.97 (RR is risk ratio; 1 means identical); 95% CI 0.94–1.00 the confidence range. Trials starting after onset found "no consistent effect" — some positive, some not. In the same review, regular daily use shortened colds by about 8% in adults (95% CI 3%–12%) and 14% in children (7%–21%). Marathon runners, skiers, and soldiers in subarctic conditions are the exception: across 5 trials, 598 people, colds fell by about half — incidence RR 0.48 (95% CI 0.35–0.64). It looks like a deal because it's cheap, sweet-sour, and the "vitamin C prevents colds" line has circulated half a century
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Hemilä H, Chalker E (2013). Vitamin C for preventing and treating the common cold. Cochrane Database Syst Rev. <https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD000980.pub4>
- Note: Contested: the same review does show regular use shortening colds — about 8% in adults (3%–12%), 14% in children (7%–21%), roughly half a day per cold. Marathon runners, skiers, and cold-region soldiers are the exception, halving colds (RR 0.48, 0.35–0.64). Almost nobody belongs to those groups

### 7. Don't order "whole-body PET-CT" or "tumor-marker panels" for yourself without symptoms
<!-- costs: money=none time=low will=no benefit=medium metric=mortality -->
- Cost: A whole-body PET-CT runs about 7,000–10,000 yuan plus a dose of ionizing radiation; tumor-marker panels a few hundred. The real cost follows: one "abnormality" that's a false alarm triggers follow-ups, biopsies, surgery — the money and misery live there.
- In plain terms: In symptom-free people, what screening finds most is false alarms. Over 80% of whole-body CT recipients had at least one "abnormality"; nearly 40% were called back; most were benign. Women screened for ovarian cancer markers died of ovarian cancer slightly more often — and some got a pointless surgery out of a false alarm.
- Benefit: The US grading body USPSTF gives CA-125 plus ultrasound screening in symptom-free women a D — recommend against — based on the PLCO trial: ovarian cancer deaths 0.34% screened vs 0.29% usual care, RR 1.18 (95% CI 0.82–1.71). No benefit. False-positive surgery rates ran 0.2%–3.25% across trials, with up to 15% of those patients suffering serious surgical complications. Another study gave 1,192 symptom-free people whole-body CT: 86% had at least one "abnormality," 37% were referred for further workup — "most findings described as benign." It looks like a deal because "scan everything at once, catch it early" fits intuition exactly
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: US Preventive Services Task Force (2018). Screening for Ovarian Cancer: US Preventive Services Task Force Recommendation Statement. JAMA（同一份建议另有 USPSTF 官方页）. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2017.21926>, <https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/recommendation/ovarian-cancer-screening>；Furtado CD et al. (2005). Whole-body CT screening: spectrum of findings and recommendations in 1192 patients. Radiology. <https://doi.org/10.1148/radiol.2372041741>
- Note: The tumor-marker half has randomized trials behind it. The whole-body CT and PET-CT half rests on follow-up records without controls — one grade weaker, effectively B. The screenings that do have evidence — colorectal, cervical, breast, low-dose chest CT for the high-risk — are another matter: Section 1. People with symptoms, or already diagnosed, getting PET-CT: that's diagnosis, not screening

### 8. Don't count on fitness-band step tracking to lose weight
<!-- costs: money=none time=low will=no benefit=medium metric=money -->
- Cost: Devices run 200–2,000 yuan, plus daily attention spent on the numbers.
- In plain terms: Overweight people on identical diet-and-exercise programs: those wearing trackers lost 3.5 kg over two years; those without lost 5.9 kg. For weight loss, the device doesn't help — the exercise does.
- Benefit: The IDEA trial took 471 overweight or obese adults for 24 months, identical diet and exercise plans, differing only in the wearable. The device group lost 3.5 kg (95% CI 2.6–4.5, the confidence range); the no-device group lost 5.9 kg (5.0–6.8) — more. Difference 2.4 kg (1.0–3.7, P=0.002). It looks like a deal because "what gets measured gets managed" sounds right, and the device is a one-time buy
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Jakicic JM et al. (2016). Effect of Wearable Technology Combined With a Lifestyle Intervention on Long-term Weight Loss: The IDEA Randomized Clinical Trial. JAMA. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2016.12858>
- Note: One trial, with early-2010s armband devices — this doesn't license "all wearables are useless." Exercise itself works: Section 2. The only conclusion: for weight loss, the device isn't a necessary expense

### 9. Don't pay the organic premium "for health"
<!-- costs: money=none time=low will=no benefit=medium metric=money -->
- Cost: Typically 1–3 times the ordinary price.
- In plain terms: No strong evidence organic food is more nutritious. Only 3 studies looked at human health outcomes after eating — none found a difference. Organic produce does show 30 percentage points lower detectable pesticide residue, but detection isn't exceeding limits. Eating more vegetables and fruit helps; organic or not doesn't change that.
- Benefit: A systematic review combined 17 human studies with 223 food-composition studies, concluding "lack of strong evidence that organic foods are significantly more nutritious than conventional." Only 3 of the human studies examined health after consumption (allergies and symptomatic infections) — no differences. Organic produce showed 30 points lower detection of pesticide residue; detection isn't exceeding limits. It looks like a deal because "no pesticides = safer = healthier," each step sounding like common sense
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Smith-Spangler C et al. (2012). Are organic foods safer or healthier than conventional alternatives?: a systematic review. Ann Intern Med. <https://doi.org/10.7326/0003-4819-157-5-201209040-00007>
- Note: Studies directly measuring human health outcomes are scarce, so the conclusion is "no evidence it's better," not "proven no benefit." Environmental impact, animal welfare, and taste aren't judged here. More vegetables and fruit helps regardless of organic status

### 10. Don't spend big on health supplements, herbal pastes, and tonics to "regulate the body"
<!-- costs: money=none time=low will=no benefit=large metric=money -->
- Cost: From a few hundred to over ten thousand yuan. Gift packaging often multiplies the price several-fold.
- In plain terms: The common ingredients — multivitamins, fish oil, vitamin D — were checked one by one in the first six items of this section: no demonstrated benefit. The line on the box, "health food is not medicine and cannot replace medication," is required by the regulator. The money mostly buys gifts for parents carried to their door — the affection is fine; the object is wrong.
- Benefit: What you get back is the money. The reasoning is in items 1–6: the most common supplement ingredients — multivitamins, fish oil, vitamin D, antioxidants, glucosamine, vitamin C — showed no expected benefit in randomized trials; each figure sits in those items' Benefit columns. Regulations govern what claims may be made; they aren't proof of uselessness. The regulator requires health foods to devote at least 20% of the front label to: "health food is not medicine and cannot replace medication." The same rules confine their purposes to "supplementing dietary nutrients, maintaining or improving health status, or reducing disease risk factors." It looks like a deal because "doesn't treat, just regulates" can never quite be proven wrong, and gift-buying runs on sentiment, not evidence
- Evidence grade: C
- Sources: 国家市场监督管理总局 (2019). 市场监管总局就《保健食品标注警示用语指南》和《保健食品原料目录与保健功能目录管理办法》有关情况举办专题新闻发布会. <https://www.samr.gov.cn/tssps/sjdt/tpxw/art/2023/art_4b658b824b1b4b0ba57c09a56cc93aad.html>
- Note: The conclusion is "no supporting evidence," not "proven ineffective" — hence C. For products with clearly listed ingredients, look up the matching item above. Those with confirmed deficiency or in treatment follow their doctor. What's rejected is the product, not the affection. If an elder already bought and takes something, and it neither replaces medication nor causes harm, don't pick that fight at their door — booking the vaccines and screenings matters more. The same money converts to things with sources in this book: annual flu shot (Section 1, item 20); shingles vaccine after 50 (item 21); pneumococcal after 65 (item 22); a blood-pressure cuff and helping them stay on medication to target (item 7); bathroom and stair retrofits plus balance training (item 13); age-appropriate cancer screenings accompanied (items 17–19); for the bed-bound, pressure-ulcer care and long-term care insurance (Section 17, items 7–8). Bring fruit, rice, and what they love instead of herbal paste. What truly must be blocked: prepaid "investment retirement" schemes, and products that displace real medication — Section 17, item 5

### 11. Don't take probiotics long-term without a gut problem
<!-- costs: money=none time=low will=no benefit=small metric=money -->
- Cost: About 2–5 yuan a day, 700–1,800 a year.
- In plain terms: Pooling 45 trials in healthy adults, probiotics don't durably change gut flora — stop, and it reverts — and blood lipids don't improve. Antibiotic-associated diarrhea and other specific situations have separate evidence; not covered here.
- Benefit: A review pooling 45 healthy-adult trials concluded "no support for durable change to gut microbiota, nor improved lipids in healthy adults." Flora does shift — temporarily, reverting on cessation. It looks like a deal because "gut health" is elastic enough to fit anything, and a bottle a day costs little
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Khalesi S et al. (2019). A review of probiotic supplementation in healthy adults: helpful or hype? Eur J Clin Nutr. <https://doi.org/10.1038/s41430-018-0135-9>
- Note: The same review found minor improvements in healthy people — better-formed stools, more vaginal lactobacilli in women. Antibiotic-associated diarrhea and specific strains for specific conditions have their own evidence. This item targets daily supplement use by the healthy

### 12. Don't force cold showers to "boost immunity"
<!-- costs: money=none time=low will=no benefit=small metric=money -->
- Cost: Free. 30–90 daily seconds of misery; winters take willpower. People with cardiovascular disease should beware — cold stress carries risk for them.
- In plain terms: In a 3,018-person trial, cold-shower takers called in sick 29% less often — but total sick days matched everyone else's, so they weren't less ill. A 2025 pool of 11 trials found inflammatory markers briefly rose after cold water, immune markers unchanged. Enjoy them if you like; just don't book them as a health investment.
- Benefit: A Dutch randomized trial, 3,018 people, ended hot showers with 30, 60, or 90 seconds of cold. Sick-leave episodes fell 29% (IRR 0.71, an episode-rate ratio). But "no significant difference in sick days": total days ill were the same. Quality of life, work productivity, and anxiety showed no meaningful differences. A 2025 systematic review pooled 11 randomized trials, 3,177 people: immediately and one hour after cold immersion, inflammatory markers rose; immune markers unchanged; stress lower only at the 12-hour point. The authors themselves call the evidence "limited by few RCTs, small samples." It looks like a deal because it's free and "suffering must work" flows too easily
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Buijze GA et al. (2016). The Effect of Cold Showering on Health and Work: A Randomized Controlled Trial. PLOS ONE. <https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0161749>；Cain T et al. (2025). Effects of cold-water immersion on health and wellbeing: A systematic review and meta-analysis. PLOS ONE. <https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0317615>
- Note: Contested: the 29% fewer sick-leave episodes doesn't look like chance, but total days ill didn't fall — more likely "went to work anyway" than "less illness." Cold-water recovery for athletes is a different question. Cold showers don't harm healthy people — just don't count them as investment

### 13. Don't buy "detox," colon-cleanse, enzyme, or alkaline-water products
<!-- costs: money=none time=low will=no benefit=medium metric=money -->
- Cost: Hundreds to thousands of yuan. The fasting-based "detoxes" can also drive blood sugar low and nutrition short.
- In plain terms: The detox industry is huge, the clinical evidence for it tiny — nobody has even trialed the commercial detox diets properly. On dietary acid-base, alkaline water, and cancer, exactly one study qualified — and found nothing.
- Benefit: That review's conclusion: "despite the detox industry's rapid growth, clinical evidence is scarce," with "no randomized controlled trials of commercial detox diets." A separate systematic review on dietary acid load, alkaline water, and cancer screened 8,278 papers and admitted one qualifying study — no association. The authors wrote that "promotion of alkaline diets and alkaline water for cancer prevention or treatment is not justified." It looks like a deal because "toxins must be flushed" and "acidity causes cancer" sound like physiology
- Evidence grade: C
- Sources: Klein AV, Kiat H (2015). Detox diets for toxin elimination and weight management: a critical review of the evidence. J Hum Nutr Diet. <https://doi.org/10.1111/jhn.12286>；Fenton TR, Huang T (2016). Systematic review of the association between dietary acid load, alkaline water and cancer. BMJ Open. <https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjopen-2015-010438>
- Note: Only "no supporting evidence" — hence C. Clearing metabolic waste is what the liver and kidneys already do. Absent disease in those organs, no extra "detox" is needed

### 14. Don't force water on yourself to hit "8 glasses a day"
<!-- costs: money=none time=low will=no benefit=small metric=money -->
- Cost: Free. A little effort remembering, and more nighttime bathroom trips.
- In plain terms: Someone chased the origin of "8 glasses a day" and found not one supporting study. Food and other drinks carry plenty of water. Drink when thirsty; check your urine color. People working heat, exercising hard, stone-formers, and elders with blunted thirst should drink proactively.
- Benefit: A review examined the "8×8" claim — 8 glasses of 8 ounces daily — concluding "no scientific studies were found" supporting it, and that surveys of thousands of adults' food and fluid intake "strongly suggest" such volumes are unnecessary, since food and other beverages already supply much water. It looks sensible because water is free, 8 is tidy, and "drink more water" offends no one
- Evidence grade: C
- Sources: Valtin H (2002). "Drink at least eight glasses of water a day." Really? Is there scientific evidence for "8 x 8"? Am J Physiol Regul Integr Comp Physiol. <https://doi.org/10.1152/ajpregu.00365.2002>
- Note: What's rejected is the mandatory count, not drinking water. Thirst plus urine color suffices for most. Heat workers, heavy exercisers, stone-formers, and thirst-dulled elders drink proactively — separate matter

### 15. Don't pay for fortune-telling, tarot, or horoscopes to make decisions
<!-- costs: money=none time=low will=no benefit=medium metric=money -->
- Cost: Free. The hard part is giving up a shortcut for decisions and thinking for yourself.
- In plain terms: In the classic classroom experiment, the "personal personality analysis" students received was identical for everyone — and most rated it accurate. That's why fortune-telling feels right. Astrologers pick correctly about as often as chance. The cost exceeds a few dozen yuan: one Henan fortune-telling ring collected incense money and "offering packages" up to 3,888 yuan; the ringleader drew eleven years.
- Benefit: The classic experiment: students took a personality test and received a "personal analysis" that was the same for all — most rated it accurate. The author concluded that "acceptance by the subject or analyst does not validate the interpretation." Of 39 students scoring accuracy 0–5, 34 gave 4 or 5, averaging about 4.26 by the table's distribution. That's the Barnum effect — why fortune-telling always feels apt. Astrology was double-blind tested in Nature: astrologers had to pick which of three personality profiles matched a natal chart. Of 116 first choices, 40 hit; pure chance yields 38.5; the astrologers themselves predicted 58.5. Official case: a Henan Xixian prosecutorial notice described an online fortune-telling fraud ring — free palm reading as bait, then incense money and "offering packages" up to 3,888 yuan; 50-odd victims, 2 million-plus yuan recovered, ringleader sentenced to 11 years plus a 100,000-yuan fine
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Forer BR (1949). The fallacy of personal validation: A classroom demonstration of gullibility. Journal of Abnormal and Social Psychology 44(1):118-123 (Table 1 rating distribution: one 2, four 3s, eighteen 4s, sixteen 5s, N=39). <https://doi.org/10.1037/h0059240>；Carlson S (1985). A double-blind test of astrology. Nature 318:419-425 (Table 2: 116 first choices, 40 hits, chance expectation 38.5, astrologers' self-prediction 58.5; "The data are consistent with chance, inconsistent with astrological hypothesis"). <https://doi.org/10.1038/318419a0>, second author's site <https://muller.lbl.gov/papers/Astrology-Carlson.pdf>；最高人民检察院 (2026). 河南息县：历经三年持续追踪打掉一批网络算命诈骗团伙. <https://www.spp.gov.cn/zdgz/202607/t20260728_732935.shtml>

### 17. Don't treat blood donation as a health practice: "detox, lipid-lowering, heart protection" lacks trial support, and frequent donation genuinely depletes iron

- Cost: Free. One donation — paperwork, draw, and rest — takes about an hour. The body spends weeks replacing the iron.
- In plain terms: Donation "detoxing," lipid-lowering, and heart protection don't hold. People able to donate repeatedly are healthier to begin with; strip that away and the mortality benefit of one extra donation yearly shrinks from nearly 20% to 7.5%. In an actual randomized comparison, deaths didn't differ. Conversely, donating too often genuinely causes fatigue and cold intolerance — iron deficiency.
- Benefit: Finland's Kuopio study followed 2,862 men aged 42–60, nearly 9 years on average. Of 153 who had donated in the prior 24 months, 1 (0.7%) had an acute myocardial infarction; of 2,529 non-donors, 316 (12.5%) did (P<0.0001). Adjusted for age and other coronary risk factors, donors ran about 88% lower infarction risk — RR 0.12 (95% CI 0.02–0.86, P=0.035). Observational, no assignment. The Nordic SCANDAT database holds 1,182,495 donors, 9,526,627 person-years, 15,401 deaths. Adjusting only for age and sex, each extra annual donation meant 18.6% lower mortality (95% CI 16.8%–20.4%); additionally adjusting for the "healthy enough to keep donating" effect left only 7.5% (5.7%–9.4%). Quality of life, physical activity, and cognition showed no differences; donation-related symptoms increased — fatigue, breathlessness, faintness, dizziness, restless legs — with lower mean hemoglobin and ferritin and more deferrals for low hemoglobin (all P<0.0001). ### 19. Don't start urate-lowering drugs because a checkup found high uric acid — if it has never hurt

### 24. Don't spend more on housing, cars, or circles to "move up a notch" among the people around you
<!-- costs: money=none time=low will=some benefit=medium metric=money -->
- Cost: Free. Just one extra question before raising the budget: am I buying the thing, or my position in this group? The hard part is not following when everyone around upgrades.
- In plain terms: Among people of equal income, the more neighbors earn, the lower one's reported well-being. This happens only with neighbors of equal education whom you actually interact with. So money spent moving somewhere everyone is richer buys you a demotion. But when incomes rise together, people still feel better — absolute levels count.
- Benefit: The US National Survey of Families and Households revisited the same people twice; the author matched them to local average income (PUMA, from the 1990 census 5% sample). Netting out own income and personal characteristics: higher neighbor earnings, lower self-reported happiness. The effect is large, robust to alternative estimation, and highly significant. In the author's phrasing, neighbors' earnings gains depress well-being on the same order of magnitude as equivalent own-earnings losses. With individual fixed effects (comparing people to themselves over time), the coefficient keeps sign and size with significance at the 10% level; with state-and-wave fixed effects, results barely change — so it's neither "people who like moving somewhere poorer are happier" nor unmeasured state factors. The effect is stronger among those who interact with neighbors and insignificant among those whose friends are all outside the neighborhood. Renters and owners show no difference — undermining the "expensive areas leave less real income" explanation. Well-being falls only with the earnings of equally educated neighbors, barely responding to others'. It transmits mainly through satisfaction with one's own material situation, not family life. And when own and neighbors' incomes rise proportionally, well-being still rises

### 31. Don't count on AI chatbots to judge your own illness or legal questions — before seeing a doctor, suing, or spending big, trace every citation they give to the source
<!-- costs: money=none time=low will=some benefit=small metric=mortality -->
- Cost: Free. Each verification costs minutes to half an hour. Have the AI name its statutes, guidelines, or papers, open the originals yourself, and find the sentence. The habit to break: trusting confident phrasing.
- In plain terms: Hand an AI a complete case and it recognizes the condition over 90% of the time. But laypeople using it for symptoms identified conditions less often than those who just searched the web — and triage judgments didn't improve either. On legal questions, general-purpose AI fabricates in at least 58% of answers, and it happily follows wrong premises embedded in your question.
- Benefit: Medicine first: a 2026 Nature Medicine randomized, preregistered trial with 1,298 UK laypeople, each judging two scripted cases — diagnosis and emergency-versus-watch. Some used AI chatbots (GPT-4o, Llama 3, Command R+); controls used their usual methods, mostly web search and the NHS site. Handed the complete case, AI identified the relevant condition 94.9% of the time and chose the right care setting 56.3%. Laypeople using the same AI: under 34.5% identified the condition, under 44.2% chose right. Controls were 1.76 times more likely than AI users to identify the relevant condition (95% CI 1.45–2.13) and 1.57× for danger-sign illnesses (1.28–1.92); triage accuracy was similar. Reading the transcripts, failure came from both ends: users under-reporting symptoms, and AI naming the right condition the user then ignored. Law next: a US team probed public models on federal-court case law in 2024 — ChatGPT 4 fabricated in 58% of answers, Llama 2 in 88%; fabrication meaning content inconsistent with legal fact, "hallucination" in the paper's terms. Models can't tell which sentence is invented. Given a false legal premise in the question, they often adopt it. The same team's 2025 test of professional legal AI tools advertising hallucination-freeness found fabrication in 17%–33%. It looks sensible because AI is always on, free, and talks like a doctor or lawyer
- Evidence grade: A
