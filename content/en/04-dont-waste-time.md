# 4. Don't waste time

This section counts only time: hours saved per day, week, or year, or the improvement in the share of things finished on schedule. Time is not converted into lifespan or money here. Where money is involved, only the method is given, not a verdict. Most entries rest on single studies or time-use survey aggregates, so grades run mostly B and C.

### 1. Write "I intend to do X" as "at what time, where, and in response to what"
<!-- costs: money=none time=low will=no benefit=large metric=time -->
- Cost: Free. One to two minutes per item, writing one "if… then…" sentence — say, "Tuesday 8 a.m., sitting down at the desk, I start with the introduction."
- In plain terms: Rewrite "I'll do this thing" as "Tuesday 8 a.m., at the desk, I start with the introduction." Across 94 tests pooled, people who write it that way hit their goals clearly more often — a medium-to-large effect. All that changed is the phrasing of one sentence: free, effortless.
- Benefit: Pooling 94 independent tests, people who first wrote intentions as "if… then…" achieved their goals more often. The format is called implementation intentions in psychology. Effect size d = 0.65, medium-to-large. It helps at three points: getting started, holding firm when interrupted, and withdrawing from doomed efforts.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Gollwitzer, P. M., & Sheeran, P. (2006). Implementation intentions and goal achievement: A meta-analysis of effects and processes. Advances in Experimental Social Psychology, 38, 69–119. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S0065-2601(06)38002-1>
- Note: This works only for things you genuinely want. If the goal itself isn't yours, no number of sentences helps. Also, these studies mostly recruited students for health habits; transferring them to work settings isn't guaranteed.

### 2. Write down your exit conditions before starting
<!-- costs: money=none time=low will=some benefit=large metric=time -->
- Cost: Free. Five minutes at kickoff, writing "if metric X isn't met by date Y, I stop." The hard part is honoring it on the day — stopping in front of others stings a little.
- In plain terms: Before starting, write one sentence: "If by date X I haven't hit indicator Y, I stop." Pooled studies confirm this format genuinely helps people withdraw from doomed efforts. What you save is all the time you would otherwise keep pouring in.
- Benefit: Pooling implementation-intention studies: pre-written "if… then…" plans also help people disengage from failing courses of action — the paper's phrase. The time saved is what you would otherwise keep investing.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Gollwitzer, P. M., & Sheeran, P. (2006). Implementation intentions and goal achievement: A meta-analysis of effects and processes. Advances in Experimental Social Psychology, 38, 69–119. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S0065-2601(06)38002-1>
- Note: Set exit conditions at kickoff, while calm. Setting them later gets bent by costs already sunk. Judge only on future investment versus future return — item 3. Also, "willingness to withdraw" is one sub-finding in a pooled analysis; nobody has separately computed the hours it saves.

### 3. When deciding whether to continue, count only future investment and future return — not what's already spent
<!-- costs: money=none time=low will=some benefit=medium metric=time -->
- Cost: Free. The hard part is accepting that the time already invested is gone and unrecoverable.
- In plain terms: Money and time already spent make you want to keep going. Across 98 data sets pooled, the tendency is robust. So to decide whether to continue, count only what more you'd put in and what you'd get back. What's already spent: leave it out.
- Benefit: Money, effort, and time already invested make people more likely to continue — the sunk-cost effect in psychology. Pooling 98 data sets confirms the tendency is stable. Avoiding it means you stop investing time just to "not waste" what's gone.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Arkes, H. R., & Blumer, C. (1985). The psychology of sunk cost. Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes, 35, 124–140. <https://doi.org/10.1016/0749-5978(85)90049-4>；Roth, S., Robbert, T., & Straus, L. (2015). On the sunk-cost effect in economic decision-making: A meta-analytic review. Business Research, 8(1), 99–138. <https://doi.org/10.1007/s40685-014-0014-8>
- Note: The Arkes and Blumer theater experiment: Ohio University theater sold season tickets at random for the full $15, $2 off, or $7 off. In the first half-season, the full-price group attended 4.11 shows on average, the others 3.32 and 3.29. By the second half, the groups no longer differed. Roth et al. found the effect fades with time, and older people are less swayed.

### 4. Estimate durations from how long similar past tasks actually took, not from the plan
<!-- costs: money=none time=low will=no benefit=large metric=time -->
- Cost: Free. A few minutes before estimating, checking how long the last similar thing actually took.
- In plain terms: Students estimated 33.9 days to finish their theses; they took 55.5, and only 29.7% finished by their own estimate. Another group was asked to recall how long a similar past task took before estimating — on-time completion rose from 29.3% to 60.0%.
- Benefit: Students predicted 33.9 days for their theses; actual was 55.5. Only 29.7% finished by their own estimate. The researchers had another group connect the task to past experience before estimating. On-time completion rose from the control's 29.3% to 60.0%. The optimism that shrinks estimates vanished in that group.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Buehler, R., Griffin, D., & Ross, M. (1994). Exploring the "planning fallacy": Why people underestimate their task completion times. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 67(3), 366–381. <https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.67.3.366>；Flyvbjerg, B. (2006). From Nobel Prize to project management: Getting risks right. Project Management Journal, 37(3), 5–15. <https://doi.org/10.1177/875697280603700302>；Halkjelsvik, T., & Jørgensen, M. (2012). From origami to software development: A review of studies on judgment-based predictions of performance time. Psychological Bulletin, 138(2), 238–271. <https://doi.org/10.1037/a0025996>
- Note: Buehler used Canadian students only — one study, small numbers: 37 and about 40 per group. Estimating from historical data of similar projects is called reference-class forecasting; how engineers do it is in Flyvbjerg. Halkjelsvik's review notes underestimation is more common than overestimation, but the size varies widely by task and method.

### 5. Circulate an agenda before meetings; no agenda, no meeting — and stand if you can
<!-- costs: money=none time=low will=no benefit=medium metric=time -->
- Cost: Free. The organizer spends 5–10 minutes writing an agenda. Standing meetings need participants' cooperation.
- In plain terms: Meetings with pre-circulated agendas are likelier to feel useful to participants. In an experiment, seated meetings ran 34% longer than standing ones — with no difference in decision quality.
- Benefit: Two surveys, 958 and 292 people: meetings with pre-circulated agendas were likelier to be perceived as effective, a statistically solid relationship. In another experiment, 55 groups met seated, 56 standing; seated meetings lasted 34% longer. Decision quality didn't differ.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Leach, D. J., Rogelberg, S. G., Warr, P. B., & Burnfield, J. L. (2009). Perceived meeting effectiveness: The role of design characteristics. Journal of Business and Psychology, 24(1), 65–76. <https://doi.org/10.1007/s10869-009-9092-6>；Bluedorn, A. C., Turban, D. B., & Love, M. S. (1999). The effects of stand-up and sit-down meeting formats on meeting outcomes. Journal of Applied Psychology, 84(2). <https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.84.2.277>
- Note: The standing experiment used 5-person lab groups. Long meetings and large conferences don't transfer. Leach measured perceived effectiveness, not output.

### 6. Cut meeting count; anything solvable asynchronously in text doesn't need a meeting
<!-- costs: money=none time=low will=some benefit=large metric=time -->
- Cost: Free. Needs colleague and boss cooperation. The hard part: writing a clear paragraph takes more effort than opening your mouth.
- In plain terms: The more meetings in a day, the more drained you feel and the heavier your workload feels. Cut one meeting and you save its full length — no discount.
- Benefit: Two surveys of employees working 35+ hours weekly, 676 and 304 people. How much time meetings take doesn't have a fixed effect on attitudes and well-being; it depends on meeting quality and how interdependent the job is. A diary study across one week: more meetings per day meant more fatigue and higher perceived workload. Time saved equals exactly the length of the meetings you cut.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Rogelberg, S. G., Leach, D. J., Warr, P. B., & Burnfield, J. L. (2006). "Not another meeting!" Are meeting time demands related to employee well-being? Journal of Applied Psychology, 91(1). <https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.91.1.83>；Luong, A., & Rogelberg, S. G. (2005). Meetings and more meetings: The relationship between meeting load and the daily well-being of employees. Group Dynamics: Theory, Research, and Practice, 9(1). <https://doi.org/10.1037/1089-2699.9.1.58>
- Note: Contested: Rogelberg 2006 found the meeting–well-being link isn't one-sided; in highly interdependent jobs, many meetings aren't bad per se. And no study has directly compared "switch to async text" against meetings — that step is the author's inference.

### 7. Break big tasks into subtasks before estimating and starting
<!-- costs: money=none time=low will=no benefit=medium metric=time -->
- Cost: Free. The splitting itself takes 5–15 minutes.
- In plain terms: People don't automatically unpack multi-part tasks when estimating. List the subtasks first and your estimate shrinks less. Once split, each small step is immediately startable — and procrastination loses its grip.
- Benefit: When estimating, people don't spontaneously split a multi-part task — literature review, discussion, references are really several tasks. Listing subtasks first reduces the underestimation error called the planning fallacy. Once split, each step becomes an action you can begin at once, and procrastination drops. The strongest predictors of procrastination in research include task aversiveness and the task being pushed later.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Kruger, J., & Evans, M. (2004). If you don't want to be late, enumerate: Unpacking reduces the planning fallacy. Journal of Experimental Social Psychology, 40(5), 586–598. <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jesp.2003.11.001>；Steel, P. (2007). The nature of procrastination: A meta-analytic and theoretical review of quintessential self-regulatory failure. Psychological Bulletin, 133(1). <https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.133.1.65>
- Note: How fine to split: each subtask should fit in one sitting; over-splitting wastes time itself. Also, Kruger and Evans was verified from the abstract for direction only — the specific percentages weren't checked against the full text.

### 8. Set your own date for things without external deadlines
<!-- costs: money=none time=low will=some benefit=large metric=time -->
- Cost: Free. The hard part is honoring it — a date you ignore on the day equals no date.
- In plain terms: With a deadline set by others, 80.6% of students finish in time. Only 38.7% finish within their own predicted time. When you finish is almost entirely set by the deadline, barely by your estimate. So for things without deadlines, set one — and honor it.
- Benefit: Students with external deadlines: 80.6% finished in time. Those finishing within their own prediction: 38.7%. Completion tracks the deadline almost completely, correlation r = 0.82 — the closer to 1, the tighter the tracking. Own predictions: only a weak relationship.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Buehler, R., Griffin, D., & Ross, M. (1994). Exploring the "planning fallacy": Why people underestimate their task completion times. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 67(3), 366–381. <https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.67.3.366>
- Note: Tell others your self-set date and it approaches the externally-set kind. Note the research validates other-imposed deadlines; self-imposed ones — timeboxing, Pomodoro — haven't been directly tested. This is extrapolation.

### 9. Treat procrastination as avoiding discomfort, not as insufficient effort
<!-- costs: money=none time=low will=some benefit=medium metric=time -->
- Cost: Free. What changes is the direction of judgment: first ask what about the task hurts, then act.
- In plain terms: Procrastination has little to do with laziness or character. What best predicts it is how aversive the task feels, whether you believe you can do it, and how easily you're pulled away by the immediate. So the useful move is making the task less aversive and the first step smaller — not scolding yourself and charging anyway.
- Benefit: A meta-analysis of 691 correlations found strong, stable links with procrastination for: task aversiveness, time until deadline, self-efficacy (believing you can), impulsivity, and conscientiousness with its facets — self-control, distractibility, organization, achievement motivation. Neuroticism, rebelliousness, and sensation-seeking showed only weak links. The author explains the pattern with temporal motivation theory: a task's present appeal shrinks fast as its reward recedes.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Steel P (2007). The nature of procrastination: a meta-analytic and theoretical review of quintessential self-regulatory failure. Psychological Bulletin, 133(1), 65–94. <https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.133.1.65>
- Note: Benefit is "medium" because these studies give direction only — no hours-saved measurement. The concrete moves are item 1 (write when-where-what), item 7 (split into subtasks), item 8 (set your own date). These are pooled correlations — no causation implied.

### 10. Put what you need within reach and what you avoid far away — don't count on in-the-moment willpower
<!-- costs: money=none time=low will=no benefit=medium metric=time -->
- Cost: Free, a few minutes once. Phone to another room; book and computer open on the desk.
- In plain terms: Things a little farther away get taken a little less. The same snacks moved farther away get eaten clearly less — a medium effect. Fewer options on display, clearly fewer takers. None of this needs in-the-moment willpower; a few minutes of setup does it.
- Benefit: A Cochrane review including 24 randomized trials. Placing food farther away reduced consumption: standardized mean difference −0.60 (95% CI −0.84 to −0.36, the confidence range; 12 studies, 1,098 people, low-certainty evidence) — a medium effect. Reducing available options cut selection of the food even more: SMD −1.13 (95% CI −1.90 to −0.37, 3 studies, 154 people, low-certainty). Regression showed: farther away and fewer alternatives, stronger the effect.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Hollands GJ, Carter P, Anwer S, et al. (2019). Altering the availability or proximity of food, alcohol, and tobacco products to change their selection and consumption. Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, 9, CD012573. <https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD012573.pub3>
- Note: Why B: all 24 included studies concerned food — none on alcohol or tobacco; 14 were lab studies, all from high-income countries. So "farther away, less touched" carried to phones and games is extrapolation, not direct trial. The one directly measured piece — phone out of sight — is Section 3, item 1 (turn off notifications; phone out of sight while working).

### 11. To make yourself follow through, stake money — but first ask whether you'd accept the stake
<!-- costs: money=low time=low will=some benefit=medium metric=time -->
- Cost: Deposit-style programs require putting up your own money, forfeited on failure. The trial staked $150.
- In plain terms: To force yourself, you can take a reward for succeeding, or post a deposit returned only on success plus a bonus. Among people willing to do both, deposit-takers outperform by 13 percentage points. But only 13.7% accept deposits while 90% accept rewards — deposits work only for those who'll take them.
- Benefit: A US randomized trial of 2,538 people assigned employees and their family or friends to four incentive designs or usual care. Reward-type (about $800 for success) acceptance was 90.0%; deposit-type ($150 up front plus a $650 bonus), 13.7%. Six-month sustained abstinence: 9.4%–16.0% across the four designs versus 6.0% usual care. Reward-type 15.7%, deposit-type 10.2%. But among only the 13.7% willing to enter either, deposit-type beat reward-type by 13.2 percentage points at 6 months (95% CI 3.1 to 22.8, the confidence range).
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Halpern SD, French B, Small DS, et al. (2015). Randomized trial of four financial-incentive programs for smoking cessation. New England Journal of Medicine, 372(22), 2108–2117. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1414293>
- Note: The trial measured quitting smoking; carrying it to theses and gym attendance is extrapolation — hence "medium." When choosing, first check whether you'd actually use the design. A method most people refuse doesn't help those people, however strong its effect. Pick the kind you'd accept; a small stake beats none. For quitting smoking itself, see Section 2, item 3 (cessation medication).

### 12. Count new habits in months, not "21 days"
<!-- costs: money=none time=low will=yes benefit=medium metric=time -->
- Cost: Free. What it takes: repeating the same action in the same context daily, for months.
- In plain terms: "21 days to build a habit" has no basis. A 12-week tracking study found an action becomes automatic in as little as 18 days or as long as 254. Still feeling effortful after a few weeks is normal. Missing one day doesn't reset progress.
- Benefit: 96 volunteers each picked an eating, drinking, or activity behavior to perform daily in a fixed context ("after breakfast") for 12 weeks, self-rating automaticity daily. 82 provided analyzable data; the model fit 62. Days to reach 95% of each person's automaticity ceiling ranged from 18 to 254. Missing one performance opportunity had no material impact on habit formation.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Lally P, van Jaarsveld CHM, Potts HWW, Wardle J (2010). How are habits formed: Modelling habit formation in the real world. European Journal of Social Psychology, 40(6), 998–1009. <https://doi.org/10.1002/ejsp.674>
- Note: One study, 96 people, all simple actions (a piece of fruit after a meal). How long complex ones take — running thrice weekly, daily writing — hasn't been measured. So still struggling after two weeks isn't evidence of failure. To make repetition happen, bind the action to a fixed context — item 1 (write when-where-what).

### 13. If procrastination is disrupting your life, use self-help material backed by randomized trials — no therapist required
<!-- costs: money=none time=medium will=some benefit=medium metric=time -->
- Cost: A self-help book costs a few dozen yuan; some are free. About ten weeks of investment, one chapter and one exercise weekly.
- In plain terms: Procrastination severe enough to cause suffering is treatable. Ten weeks of cognitive-behavioral self-help, guided or unguided, clearly beats nothing. Guided versus unguided showed no difference — so working through it alone works too.
- Benefit: 150 people randomized to therapist-guided self-help, unguided self-help, or waitlist. After ten weeks, between-group effect sizes on the pure procrastination scale: guided d = 0.70 (95% CI 0.29–1.10, the confidence range), unguided d = 0.50 (0.10–0.90); on the irrational procrastination scale, 0.81 (0.40–1.22) and 0.69 (0.29–1.09). Clinically significant improvement: guided 31.3%–40.0%, unguided 24.0%–36.0%. No significant difference on any outcome.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Rozental A, Forsell E, Svensson A, Andersson G, Carlbring P (2015). Internet-based cognitive-behavior therapy for procrastination: A randomized controlled trial. Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology, 83(4), 808–824. <https://doi.org/10.1037/ccp0000023>
- Note: Swedish adults, Swedish-language materials; no ready-made Chinese equivalent — hence "medium." The ingredients are the usual: behavioral activation, behavioral experiments, stimulus control, psychoeducation on motivation and work methods. Stimulus control is item 10 (move what you avoid far away). If procrastination comes with clear low mood or anxiety, start with Section 3, item 18 (move, sunlight, sleep, talk, call 12356).

### 14. Use your hourly rate to decide which chores to outsource
<!-- costs: money=high time=low will=no benefit=large metric=time -->
- Cost: The outsourcing itself costs money, depending on the service. Plus one-time effort to compute your hourly rate.
- In plain terms: Chinese people average 1 hour 17 minutes of housework daily; those who actually did housework that day, 1 hour 59. People who spend money to buy time are more satisfied with life. The same money spent buying time brings more in-the-moment happiness than buying things.
- Benefit: Chinese residents average 1 hour 17 minutes of daily housework; counting only those who did any, 1 hour 59 minutes. A 2024 survey covering 38,500 households, 107,000 people. Another study sampled 6,271 people across four countries: those spending money to buy time had higher life satisfaction. The researchers also ran real-life experiments: the same sum spent buying time brought higher momentary well-being than buying material goods.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Whillans, A. V., Dunn, E. W., Smeets, P., Bekkers, R., & Norton, M. I. (2017). Buying time promotes happiness. Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences. <https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1706541114>；国家统计局 (2024). 第三次全国时间利用调查公报（第二号）. <https://www.stats.gov.cn/sj/zxfb/202410/t20241031_1957216.html>
- Note: First compute the hourly rate: monthly after-tax income ÷ actual monthly work hours. Then price the chore's outsourcing. If the price is below your rate and you'll truly use the freed time for earning or rest, outsource. Rates and prices differ per person — do your own math. Also, Whillans measured happiness after buying time, not hours saved.

### 15. Set hard caps on short video and aimless scrolling
<!-- costs: money=none time=low will=yes benefit=large metric=time -->
- Cost: Free. The action takes minutes: kill push, delete the app, or set a daily limit. The hard part is the hand discipline — not tapping open.
- In plain terms: Chinese people average 5 hours 37 minutes online daily; those actually online that day, 6 hours 3. In 2018 the same measure showed 2 hours 42. This is the biggest number in this section. Cut one hour a day and you gain 365 hours a year.
- Benefit: Chinese residents average 5 hours 37 minutes online daily; those online that day, 6 hours 3 minutes. 92.9% used the internet that day. These are 2024 figures. In 2018 the same method showed only 2 hours 42 minutes — the biggest number in this section. Cutting one hour daily equals 365 hours a year. Per CNNIC, by June 2025 netizens averaged 30.6 hours online weekly, up 1.9 from six months before. Short-video users numbered 1.068 billion, 95.1% of netizens.
- Evidence grade: C
- Sources: 国家统计局 (2024). 第三次全国时间利用调查公报（第二号）. <https://www.stats.gov.cn/sj/zxfb/202410/t20241031_1957216.html>；国家统计局 (2019). 2018年全国时间利用调查公报. <https://www.stats.gov.cn/sj/zxfb/202302/t20230203_1900224.html>；中国互联网络信息中心 (2025). 第 56 次《中国互联网络发展状况统计报告》. <https://www.cnnic.net.cn/NMediaFile/2025/0730/MAIN1753846666507QEK67ZS9DH.pdf>
- Note: Online time includes work, study, and social contact — not all waste. Grade C because no study has measured "how much is reclaimed after capping, and where it goes" — we hold only aggregate statistics. For the "seeing how others are doing" class specifically, a randomized trial measured reclaimed time and mood: Section 3, item 20.

### 16. No TV and no scrolling news; read what you need in scheduled batches
<!-- costs: money=none time=low will=some benefit=large metric=time -->
- Cost: Free. The hard part is breaking the reflex to open it — plus possibly missing some instant news.
- In plain terms: Americans 15+ watch 2.6 hours of TV daily — half of all leisure. Chinese people average 1 hour 40 minutes; those 75–84 watch 3 hours 16. Switch to scheduled batch reading, and you reclaim the whole block.
- Benefit: Americans 15+ watch 2.6 hours of TV daily out of 5.2 hours of leisure — half. 2025 figures. Chinese residents average 1 hour 40 minutes of TV daily; ages 75–84 watch 3 hours 16 minutes (2018 figures).
- Evidence grade: C
- Sources: U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics (2026). American Time Use Survey — 2025 Results. <https://www.bls.gov/news.release/atus.nr0.htm>；国家统计局 (2019). 2018年全国时间利用调查公报. <https://www.stats.gov.cn/sj/zxfb/202302/t20230203_1900224.html>
- Note: Only aggregate statistics in hand. No study has measured where the reclaimed time goes after cutting TV and news. "Batch reading" is author experience.

### 17. Spend once on learning keyboard shortcuts and automation for software you use daily
<!-- costs: money=none time=medium will=some benefit=medium metric=time -->
- Cost: Free. A one-time investment of hours to tens of hours. The hard part: it's slower than the mouse at first.
- In plain terms: Most people who've used Word for years still click toolbar icons. In grouped experiments, shortcuts beat menus and icons for speed. Learning is a one-time cost that pays back on every operation, every day. The longer you do the same kind of work, the more it returns.
- Benefit: The study examined 251 experienced Word users; most rarely used the faster shortcuts, working mainly from icon toolbars. Grouped experiments confirmed that among menus, icons, and shortcuts, shortcuts were fastest. Time saved accumulates with daily operation count. People doing the same work long-term gain most.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Lane, D. M., Napier, H. A., Peres, S. C., & Sándor, A. (2005). Hidden costs of graphical user interfaces: Failure to make the transition from menus and icon toolbars to keyboard shortcuts. International Journal of Human-Computer Interaction. <https://doi.org/10.1207/s15327590ijhc1802_1>
- Note: Learn only operations you use ten-plus times daily; learning too broadly is its own waste. Note: the efficiency experiment had 6 participants, and the abstract gives no per-operation seconds saved.

### 18. When choosing where to live, put commute length first — shorten the one-way commute
<!-- costs: money=high time=medium will=no benefit=large metric=time -->
- Cost: High. Possibly higher rent or a smaller home. Moving itself takes time.
- In plain terms: The longer the commute, the worse people feel about their lives — and that loss is not compensated in salary or housing. Thirty minutes less one-way is 5 hours a week.
- Benefit: Chinese residents spend 50 minutes daily on transport on average; counting only those who traveled, 1 hour 2 minutes (2024). German panel data following the same people show longer commuters with stably lower subjective well-being, uncompensated by income or housing. A review across studies confirms commute time predicts commute dissatisfaction regardless of transport mode. Thirty minutes less one-way saves about 5 hours weekly.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Stutzer, A., & Frey, B. S. (2008). Stress that doesn't pay: The commuting paradox. Scandinavian Journal of Economics, 110(2), 339–366. <https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9442.2008.00542.x>；Chatterjee, K., et al. (2020). Commuting and wellbeing: A critical overview of the literature with implications for policy and future research. Transport Reviews, 40(1), 5–34. <https://doi.org/10.1080/01441647.2019.1649317>；国家统计局 (2024). 第三次全国时间利用调查公报（第二号）. <https://www.stats.gov.cn/sj/zxfb/202410/t20241031_1957216.html>
- Note: Contested: Chatterjee's review finds no stable relationship between commute length and overall life satisfaction — people largely trade the loss back via better jobs or housing. Note too that the transport statistics count all travel, not just commuting. And with off-peak hours, the same road runs shorter.
