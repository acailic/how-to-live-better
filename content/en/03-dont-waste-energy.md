# 3. Don't waste energy

This section counts only energy and time. Four things: the attention you have each day, how fast you react, how often you err, and how long it takes to come back after an interruption. All numbers are directly measured — from experiments, or from tracking groups of people (cohort studies). They are not converted into life-years or mortality. Most entries here are Grade B; that's the state of the field, not a sign the advice is wrong. Item 19 — expectations when dealing with police, doctors, and counter clerks — cites regulations, not experiments, so it's Grade C. Items 15 (treat pessimistic thoughts as a symptom) and 22 (treat "others demand I be perfect" as a symptom) cite mortality and suicide-risk numbers, a different metric from the rest of this section, so they aren't ranked alongside the others.

Entries in this section are grouped by theme, with item numbers in parentheses.

**Attention at work**: turn off non-essential notifications (item 1), batch messages a few times daily (item 5), block even seconds-long interruptions (item 6), do one thing at a time (item 7), earplugs in open offices (item 12), don't endlessly extend weekly work hours (item 13).

**Sleep and late nights**: fix your wake time (item 2), sleep 7–8 hours nightly (item 3), no caffeine after 2 p.m. (item 4), no screens the hour before bed (item 8), sleep when it's time (item 9), no big decisions late at night (item 10), nap 10 minutes when the afternoon slump hits (item 11).

**Thought loops**: switch to a hands-on task when rumination starts (item 14), treat pessimistic thoughts as a symptom (item 15), halve your estimate when you feel you embarrassed yourself (item 21), treat "others demand I be perfect" as a symptom (item 22).

**Anger and low mood**: leave the scene first when angry (item 17), first steps when mood sinks (item 18), don't vent anger by smashing things (item 23), write your worries down for several days running (item 24).

**People and social media**: learn to refuse requests you don't want (item 16), treat police and doctors as people working by rules (item 19), limit apps full of peers' updates (item 20).

**Bodily pain**: take ibuprofen-type painkillers for menstrual cramps (item 25).

### 1. Turn off non-essential notifications, and keep the phone out of sight while working
<!-- costs: money=none time=low will=no benefit=medium metric=time -->
- Cost: Free. A few minutes changing settings; almost no willpower.
- In plain terms: One phone buzz — even unseen, untouched — drops your attention-task performance. By about as much as actually taking the call or texting. Worse: with the phone just sitting on the desk, resisting the urge to look still shrinks your usable headroom. The more phone-dependent you are, the bigger the drop.
- Benefit: Merely receiving one notification, without looking or touching, measurably worsens attention-task performance — by an amount comparable to actually calling or texting. This was in a lab, with students. Two further experiments: even when people successfully resisted checking, having their own phone beside them reduced available cognitive capacity. The most phone-dependent dropped most.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Stothart, Mitchum & Yehnert (2015). The attentional cost of receiving a cell phone notification. Journal of Experimental Psychology: Human Perception and Performance. <https://doi.org/10.1037/xhp0000100>；Ward, Duke, Gneezy & Bos (2017). Brain Drain: The Mere Presence of One's Own Smartphone Reduces Available Cognitive Capacity. Journal of the Association for Consumer Research. <https://doi.org/10.1086/691462>
- Note: These are single lab tasks. Nobody has measured the cumulative effect of doing this daily for years. If your job truly requires being reachable, whitelist a handful of contacts.

### 2. Fix your wake time — weekends included
<!-- costs: money=none time=low will=some benefit=medium metric=time -->
- Cost: Free. Sleeping in less on weekends takes a little willpower.
- In plain terms: The most irregular fifth of sleepers run a body clock about 2.5 hours later than the most regular fifth — like living in permanent jet lag. The more regular the schedule, the better the grades.
- Benefit: 61 students logged 30 days. The most irregular fifth's circadian clock ran about 2.5 hours later than the most regular fifth's — specifically, melatonin onset at 00:08 versus 21:32. Their sleepiest window also fell about 1.8 hours later. The more regular the schedule, the better the academic performance (correlation r = 0.37).
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Phillips et al. (2017). Irregular sleep/wake patterns are associated with poorer academic performance and delayed circadian and sleep/wake timing. Scientific Reports. <https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-017-03171-4>
- Note: One study, few people, no control group. The researchers inferred the clock differences come mostly from differing daily light exposure — so fix your wake time together with morning daylight, or it won't work. For recovering after the occasional late night, see Section 2, item 38 (catch up the next night): go to bed early that night, not sleep till noon next day.

### 3. Sleep 7–8 hours a night — don't treat 6 as enough
<!-- costs: money=none time=high will=some benefit=large metric=time -->
- Cost: Free. Reserve 1–2 more hours for sleep daily. Going to bed earlier takes some willpower.
- In plain terms: Two weeks of 6-hour nights drags your mental performance down to the level of two full sleepless days and nights. The trouble is you can't feel it: self-rated sleepiness rises for a few days and then plateaus — 6-hour sleepers rate themselves as sleepy as 4-hour sleepers. So "6 hours is enough for me" is itself a symptom of sleep deprivation.
- Benefit: 48 healthy adults aged 21–38 slept only 6 hours nightly for 14 consecutive days. Their cognitive performance declined cumulatively to the equivalent of 2 full nights without sleep. Yet self-rated sleepiness rose only in the first days and then barely moved. The 6-hour and 4-hour groups rated themselves equally sleepy. This was a randomized controlled trial (RCT). Separately, short total deprivation (under 48 hours) affects simple-attention lapses with effect size g = −0.776 and reasoning accuracy g = −0.125. That part pools 70 studies.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Van Dongen, Maislin, Mullington & Dinges (2003). The cumulative cost of additional wakefulness. Sleep. <https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/26.2.117>；Lim & Dinges (2010). A meta-analysis of the impact of short-term sleep deprivation on cognitive variables. Psychological Bulletin. <https://doi.org/10.1037/a0018883>
- Note: "I don't feel sleepy" is not evidence of enough sleep. Long-term 6-hour sleepers have merely habituated subjectively; performance hasn't kept up. The pooled part concerns whole nights skipped. Chronic short sleep rests on one RCT. Sleep duration versus death risk is a separate account — Section 2, item 13 (about 7 hours): by mortality, the 7-hour band is lowest.

### 4. No caffeine after 2 p.m.
<!-- costs: money=none time=low will=some benefit=large metric=time -->
- Cost: Free. Afternoon-coffee drinkers need some willpower to skip it.
- In plain terms: A large coffee (400 mg) 6 hours before bed costs over an hour of instrument-measured sleep that night. Yet your own sleep diary shows no difference. An ordinary cup (107 mg) needs to be finished 8.8+ hours before bed to leave sleep alone. If you sleep at eleven, 2 p.m. is the line.
- Benefit: 400 mg of caffeine 6 hours before bed cut instrument-measured total sleep by 1.1–1.2 hours versus placebo. A placebo is a caffeine-free control drink. Yet participants' own sleep diaries showed no difference — they couldn't tell. A pooled analysis (meta-analysis): caffeine cuts total sleep by 45 minutes on average. Sleep efficiency — the share of time in bed actually asleep — falls 7%. One cup (107 mg) must be finished more than 8.8 hours before bed to avoid affecting sleep.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Drake, Roehrs, Shambroom & Roth (2013). Caffeine effects on sleep taken 0, 3, or 6 hours before going to bed. Journal of Clinical Sleep Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.5664/jcsm.3170>；Gardiner et al. (2023). The effect of caffeine on subsequent sleep: A systematic review and meta-analysis. Sleep Medicine Reviews. <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2023.101764>
- Note: The Drake study comes from one lab, and 400 mg is on the high side — two or three coffees. Slow caffeine metabolizers need an earlier cutoff. What skipping caffeine buys is sleep duration, stacking with item 3 (7–8 hours).

### 5. Batch email and messages into a few fixed times a day
<!-- costs: money=none time=low will=some benefit=medium metric=time -->
- Cost: Free. Takes some time to tell colleagues how often you reply. Resisting the constant inbox check takes some willpower.
- In plain terms: The same people compared two weeks. The week email was limited to 3 checks a day, day-to-day stress was clearly lower — with the same number of emails sent and received. Unrestricted, people checked 15.5 times a day on average.
- Benefit: 124 adults, two weeks of self-comparison (within-subject). The week with email limited to 3 daily checks showed clearly lower daily stress than the unlimited week (effect size Cohen's d = 0.37). Emails received and sent didn't differ between weeks. Unrestricted at the start, people checked email 15.5 times daily on average.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Kushlev & Dunn (2015). Checking email less frequently reduces stress. Computers in Human Behavior. <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2014.11.005>
- Note: One study, small-to-medium effect, measuring stress — not output. Instant messaging hasn't been studied directly; this is extrapolation.

### 6. When doing work that needs continuous thought, block even seconds-long interruptions
<!-- costs: money=none time=low will=no benefit=large metric=time -->
- Cost: Free. Close the door, put on headphones, or hang a do-not-disturb sign.
- In plain terms: An interruption averaging 2.8 seconds doubles the error rate on what follows. 4.4 seconds triples it. Field tracking also found the interrupted task takes 25 minutes on average to return to, with two other tasks inserted along the way. Interrupted people work faster — with higher stress, frustration, and effort.
- Benefit: Interruptions averaging 2.8 seconds double the step-error rate on sequential tasks; 4.4-second ones triple it. Lab work. A separate field study observed 24 office knowledge workers: they switched work topics every 11 minutes on average; 57% of topics were interrupted; returning to an interrupted topic took 25 minutes 26 seconds on average, with 2.26 other tasks interleaved. A 48-person experiment: interrupted people finished faster but reported more stress, frustration, time pressure, and effort.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Altmann, Trafton & Hambrick (2014). Momentary interruptions can derail the train of thought. Journal of Experimental Psychology: General. <https://doi.org/10.1037/a0030986>；Mark, Gonzalez & Harris (2005). No task left behind? Examining the nature of fragmented work. CHI 2005. <https://doi.org/10.1145/1054972.1055017>；Mark, Gudith & Klocke (2008). The cost of interrupted work: More speed and stress. CHI 2008. <https://doi.org/10.1145/1357054.1357072>
- Note: The field study split interruptions in two: people coming to you, and things you open yourself. Closing the door blocks only the first kind. The second kind needs item 1 (turn off notifications) and item 5 (batching) alongside.

### 7. Do one thing at a time — don't answer messages mid-meeting
<!-- costs: money=none time=low will=some benefit=medium metric=time -->
- Cost: Free. Not answering messages in meetings takes some willpower.
- In plain terms: Every task switch slows reactions and invites errors. Heavy multi-screeners haven't trained up an ability to do several things at once — they actually perform worse on task-switching tests.
- Benefit: After task switches, reactions are measurably slower and more error-prone — from a review of many studies. Another lab study used students: heavy media multitaskers are more easily dragged off by irrelevant stimuli and irrelevant memories, and perform worse on task-switching tests.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Monsell (2003). Task switching. Trends in Cognitive Sciences. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S1364-6613(03)00028-7>；Ophir, Nass & Wagner (2009). Cognitive control in media multitaskers. PNAS. <https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0903620106>
- Note: How much switching costs varies hugely by task; no single number offered. Ophir's is a same-time-point comparison — it can't separate "multitasking worsens attention" from "worse attention seeks multitasking."

### 8. No glowing screens the hour before bed — paper books or e-ink if you must read
<!-- costs: money=none time=low will=yes benefit=medium metric=time -->
- Cost: Free. Putting the phone down an hour before bed fights inertia daily — willpower.
- In plain terms: Reading a glowing screen before bed delays falling asleep by about 10 minutes versus paper, suppresses melatonin by 55%, and pushes the next day's body clock back over 1.5 hours — sleepier mornings, longer to fully wake.
- Benefit: A lab crossover trial — the same people under both conditions. Reading a light-emitting e-reader before bed meant falling asleep about 10 minutes slower than paper (25.7 vs 15.8 minutes). Evening melatonin suppressed 55%. The next day's circadian phase delayed by over 1.5 hours — melatonin onset at 22:31 versus 21:01. Next morning: sleepier, and taking longer to become fully alert.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Chang, Aeschbach, Duffy & Czeisler (2015). Evening use of light-emitting eReaders negatively affects sleep, circadian timing, and next-morning alertness. PNAS. <https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1418490112>
- Note: Small sample, tightly controlled lab conditions. At everyday phone brightness and durations the effect may be smaller. Whether night mode helps wasn't tested here.

### 9. Sleep when it's time — don't lose nights to games, short videos, or pornography
<!-- costs: money=none time=low will=yes benefit=large metric=time -->
- Cost: Free. The hard part is the long fight against "one more episode." Keeping the phone out of the bedroom is the cheapest way.
- In plain terms: In 2025 Chinese people slept 6.97 hours a night on average, falling asleep at 0:10. People using phones or tablets before bed run over double the risk of insufficient sleep and 2.7 times the daytime sleepiness. Even a device merely present — unused — in the bedroom shows similar numbers. So the easy fix: the phone doesn't enter the bedroom.
- Benefit: In 2025, average night sleep was 6.97 hours with sleep onset at 0:10 (Chinese Sleep Research Society). Portable-device users before bed: about 2.17 times the odds of insufficient sleep (OR 2.17). Poor sleep quality about 1.46 times (OR 1.46). Daytime sleepiness about 2.72 times (OR 2.72). A device merely present in the bedroom, unused, showed similar numbers — about 1.79, 1.53, 2.27 (OR 1.79/1.53/2.27). That part pools 20 cross-sectional surveys, 125,198 children and adolescents. Another cross-sectional survey: among 844 adults aged 18–94, phone use after lights-out meant slower sleep onset, lower sleep efficiency, and more daytime fatigue.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Carter et al. (2016). Association Between Portable Screen-Based Media Device Access or Use and Sleep Outcomes. JAMA Pediatrics. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapediatrics.2016.2341>；Exelmans & Van den Bulck (2016). Bedtime mobile phone use and sleep in adults. Social Science & Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.socscimed.2015.11.037>；中国睡眠研究会 (2026). 2026 中国睡眠健康研究白皮书（福建省卫健委转载健康报报道）. <https://wjw.fujian.gov.cn/jggk/csxx/xcc/mtbd/202603/t20260323_7114382.htm>；Grubbs JB et al. (2018). Pornography Problems Due to Moral Incongruence: An Integrative Model with a Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis. Archives of Sexual Behavior. <https://doi.org/10.1007/s10508-018-1248-x> (one side of the note's dispute); Prause N, Pfaus J (2015). Viewing Sexual Stimuli Associated with Greater Sexual Responsiveness, Not Erectile Dysfunction. Sexual Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.1002/sm2.58> (the other side)
- Note: Contested. The cost of late nights themselves is item 3 (7–8 hours). Whether pornography affects ordinary people's energy and cognition has no reliable evidence settling causation. One pooled analysis holds that "pornography problems" stem mostly from the mismatch between a person's beliefs and their own behavior, not the content itself. Another pooled 280 men and found viewing duration unrelated to erectile function with a partner. This entry targets only its theft of sleep hours. On the 6.97-hour and 0:10 figures: the Sleep Research Society's site hadn't posted the release (checked 2026-09); the Fujian health commission's reprint of the Health News report is the source of record.

### 10. No big decisions or important messages late at night
<!-- costs: money=none time=low will=some benefit=small metric=time -->
- Cost: Free. Just postponing things until after sleep.
- In plain terms: Well-rested people quickly learn to avoid high-risk options. After two sleepless nights, they keep picking the high-risk ones — more so with age. Nobody has measured what a few short hours does, but move important decisions to after you've slept.
- Benefit: 34 healthy people did the Iowa Gambling Task — a risk-decision experiment — after 49.5 hours awake. Rested, they quickly learned to avoid high-risk options. Sleep-deprived, they chose them more often. Older participants were affected more. One experiment, one study.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Killgore, Balkin & Wesensten (2006). Impaired decision making following 49 h of sleep deprivation. Journal of Sleep Research. <https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2869.2006.00487.x>
- Note: The study covers the extreme of two full nights awake. What staying up till 2 a.m. does to ordinary people wasn't directly measured. The "no messages" half is author experience (Grade C) — it can wait until tomorrow.

### 11. Nap 10 minutes when the afternoon slump hits — not half an hour
<!-- costs: money=none time=low will=no benefit=medium metric=time -->
- Cost: Free. 10 minutes, plus somewhere to lie or lean.
- In plain terms: A 10-minute nap revives you on the spot, and the effect lasts two and a half hours. 5 minutes does essentially nothing. 20 minutes takes half an hour to kick in. 30 minutes wakes you into a fog that performs worse than no nap at all.
- Benefit: After a short night, a 10-minute nap immediately improved sleepiness, fatigue, energy, and cognitive performance, with part of the benefit persisting past 155 minutes. 5 minutes: almost no benefit. 20 minutes: benefits appear only after 35 minutes. 30 minutes: an initial stretch of sleep inertia — performance worse than no nap at first, improving only later.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Brooks & Lack (2006). A brief afternoon nap following nocturnal sleep restriction: Which nap duration is most recuperative? Sleep. <https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/29.6.831>
- Note: One lab study, healthy young subjects. If you can't fall asleep, don't force it. Whether lying still with eyes closed for 10 minutes helps has no research support. Napping past an hour links to higher mortality and coronary risk — Section 2, item 37 (naps under half an hour).

### 12. In open-plan offices, use earplugs or book a quiet room for memory work
<!-- costs: money=low time=low will=no benefit=small metric=time -->
- Cost: Earplugs, a few dozen yuan. Booking a quiet room takes a little arranging.
- In plain terms: Background noise rising from 39 to 51 decibels means fewer words remembered, more fatigue, and less desire to work. On breaks, a nature film with sound restores energy better than continuing to sit in office noise.
- Benefit: Students worked in a simulated open-plan office. Raising background noise from 39 to 51 dB (LAeq) meant fewer recalled words, higher self-rated fatigue, and lower motivation. A break watching a nature film with sound restored energy ratings better than river sounds alone or continued office noise.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Jahncke, Hygge, Halin, Green & Dimberg (2011). Open-plan office noise: Cognitive performance and restoration. Journal of Environmental Psychology. <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jenvp.2011.07.002>
- Note: One lab study. The most damaging part of the noise is intelligible speech. White-noise headphones help with that, but this study didn't test them.

### 13. Don't keep piling on weekly hours: past roughly 49, each extra hour yields less and less
<!-- costs: money=low time=low will=some benefit=large metric=time -->
- Cost: Possibly less overtime pay, and possibly a conversation with your boss.
- In plain terms: WWI British munitions-factory records show output tracking hours one-for-one up to 49 hours a week. Beyond 49, each extra hour buys less and less. At 70 hours a week, output was nearly identical to 56 — those extra 14 hours were wasted.
- Benefit: WWI British munitions workers' output records: below 49 weekly hours, output proportional to hours. Past 49, output growth slows. Peak at about 63 hours. 70-hour weeks produced almost exactly what 56-hour weeks did — 14 hours for nothing.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Pencavel (2015). The Productivity of Working Hours. The Economic Journal. <https://doi.org/10.1111/ecoj.12166>
- Note: The data are century-old factory work — physical, mostly female workers. Where the threshold sits for knowledge work may differ; the author says other jobs could sit higher or lower. What's solid is the direction: past some point, extra hours add almost no output. The specific 49 is not.

### 14. When you catch yourself replaying the same bad event, switch to a hands-on task
<!-- costs: money=none time=low will=some benefit=medium metric=time -->
- Cost: Free. The hard part is noticing you're replaying it (it's called rumination) — noticing itself takes practice.
- In plain terms: Replaying the same bad event doesn't produce answers. It deepens depression, nails negative thoughts in place, makes problems less solvable and action less likely, and drains the people willing to listen. Catch yourself circling, go do something with your hands.
- Benefit: A review of many studies: rumination — replaying the same bad event — deepens depression. It reinforces negative thinking and weakens problem-solving. It obstructs actual action and exhausts supportive listeners. It correlates with anxiety, binge eating, alcohol misuse, and self-harm.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Nolen-Hoeksema, Wisco & Lyubomirsky (2008). Rethinking Rumination. Perspectives on Psychological Science. <https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1745-6924.2008.00088.x>
- Note: The review gives direction, not numbers. "Switch tasks" is the author's practice. What the literature supports is one sentence: diverting attention beats circling in place. Which task to switch to has never been compared.

### 15. Treat "things will definitely get worse" as a symptom, not a fact
<!-- costs: money=none time=low will=some benefit=medium metric=mortality -->
- Cost: Free. The hard part is seeing the thought as a symptom rather than a fact.
- In plain terms: In a 20-year twin study, each step up in pessimism added about 13% to mortality over the period, and about 20% to cardiovascular death. In the other direction, optimism scores bought no matching benefit. So the thing to treat is pessimism, not "insufficient positivity."
- Benefit: 2,978 Australian twins over 50, average 20 years of follow-up, 1,068 deaths. Each standard deviation up on the pessimism scale: about 13% higher mortality (hazard ratio 1.134), 95% CI 1.065–1.207, P=8.85×10⁻⁵. Cardiovascular death about 20% higher (1.196, 1.045–1.368, P=0.0093). No link to cancer death. Optimism scores showed no clear association with mortality either.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Whitfield JB, Zhu G, Landers JG, Martin NG (2020). Pessimism is associated with greater all-cause and cardiovascular mortality, but optimism is not protective. Scientific Reports, 10, 12609. <https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-020-69388-y>
- Note: Contested. Observation only, no assignment. One might say "illness came first and caused the pessimism"; the authors counter that cancer deaths would then correlate too — they didn't. Still, unmeasured factors can't be excluded. Optimism wasn't protective, so don't force "positive thinking." What to do is stop treating pessimistic forecasts as accurate predictions of the future.

### 16. Cut draining relationships; learn to refuse requests you don't want
<!-- costs: money=none time=low will=yes benefit=medium metric=time -->
- Cost: Free. Short-term social friction, possibly some relationships lost. Saying no takes real willpower.
- In plain terms: One draining person costs you more than one supportive person gains you — more consistently, too. So subtract before you add.
- Benefit: A survey of 120 widowed women aged 60–89: distressing interactions correlated with psychological well-being more consistently — and more strongly — than pleasant ones. On that result, one draining person's damage exceeds one supportive person's benefit.
- Evidence grade: C
- Sources: Rook (1984). The negative side of social interaction: Impact on psychological well-being. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology. <https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.46.5.1097>
- Note: A special population, one-shot questionnaire, no direction of causation. It measured well-being, not energy; placing it in this section is the author's inference. What counts as "draining" has no objective standard — your own judgment is the only one.

### 17. When angry, leave the scene first — treat the other party as weather, not as an enemy
<!-- costs: money=none time=low will=some benefit=medium metric=time -->
- Cost: Free. In the moment: bite back the retort, walk away, decide later whether to deal with it. Takes a little practice.
- In plain terms: Reinterpreting what just happened, or stepping into the other person's shoes, genuinely lowers the emotion — the second one works better. Suppressing the display does nothing. And people who punish an offender keep replaying the episode longer afterward; those who don't retaliate move on faster. People overestimate in advance how good revenge will feel.
- Benefit: Pooling 306 experimental comparisons (meta-analysis): reappraisal — reframing what happened — has effect size d = 0.36 on emotion. Perspective-taking, d = 0.45. Distraction, d = 0.27. Suppressing the feeling: ineffective (d = −0.04). Three further experiments: those who punished an offender ruminated about them longer afterward; those who didn't "turned the page" faster. People overestimate the satisfaction revenge will bring.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Webb TL, Miles E, Sheeran P (2012). Dealing with feeling: A meta-analysis of the effectiveness of strategies derived from the process model of emotion regulation. Psychological Bulletin. <https://doi.org/10.1037/a0027600>；Carlsmith KM, Wilson TD, Gilbert DT (2008). The paradoxical consequences of revenge. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology. <https://doi.org/10.1037/a0012165>
- Note: The pooled part measures emotional intensity, not energy, with small-to-medium effects. The revenge part is one set of experiments — hence B. The "empty boat" is Zhuangzi's parable: an empty boat drifts into yours and you don't get angry; a boat with someone in it, you do — though the collision is the same. That's analogy, not evidence. If there's real damage, take the legal routes of Sections 8 and 9 — far cheaper than blowing up on the spot.

### 18. When mood sinks, do the highest-value basics first: move, get sunlight, sleep on schedule, talk to someone, call 12356
<!-- costs: money=none time=medium will=some benefit=large metric=time -->
- Cost: Free or nearly. Brisk walks, jogs, or strength training several times weekly. Daylight by day. A fixed schedule. One phone call when things feel unbearable.
- In plain terms: For depression, walking and jogging work best, more intensity better — details in Section 22, item 6 (walk or run when mood is low). Light therapy is strong for seasonal depression and works for non-seasonal too. Online self-help CBT courses work; with guidance they work better at heavier symptom levels. When it feels unbearable, call 12356 first.
- Benefit: A network meta-analysis pooled 218 randomized trials, 14,170 people, against active control groups (people doing some other activity). Versus them, walking or jogging for depression: effect size g = −0.62 (95% CI −0.80 to −0.45). Yoga −0.55, strength training −0.49. Higher intensity, better effect. Pooled light-therapy studies: seasonal depression effect size 0.84 (0.60 to 1.08); non-seasonal 0.53 (0.18 to 0.89). Another analysis pooled individual patient data across studies of internet cognitive behavioral therapy (online self-help courses). Both guided and unguided beat controls. At moderate-severe levels (PHQ-9 above 9), guided was better. 12356 is the national psychological-assistance hotline, open no fewer than 18 hours daily.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Noetel M et al. (2024). Effect of exercise for depression: systematic review and network meta-analysis of randomised controlled trials. BMJ. <https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj-2023-075847>；Golden RN et al. (2005). The efficacy of light therapy in the treatment of mood disorders: a review and meta-analysis of the evidence. American Journal of Psychiatry. <https://doi.org/10.1176/appi.ajp.162.4.656>；Karyotaki E et al. (2021). Internet-Based Cognitive Behavioral Therapy for Depression: A Systematic Review and Individual Patient Data Network Meta-analysis. JAMA Psychiatry. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2020.4364>；国家卫生健康委 (2024). 关于应用"12356"全国统一心理援助热线电话号码的通知（国卫医政函〔2024〕259 号）. <https://www.gov.cn/zhengce/zhengceku/202412/content_6994470.htm>
- Note: These are adjuncts for mild-to-moderate depression. Moderate-severe, or with suicidal thoughts, means medical care — call 12356 first (Section 1, item 25). The exercise trials are mostly small, and can't blind who's exercising the way drug trials can. The light studies run only to 2003 and are small. "Sleep on schedule" and "talk to someone" are author experience without separate citations. Don't turn "move" into a demand on a depressed person — ten minutes of walking counts.

### 19. Treat police, doctors, and counter clerks as people working by rules, not as roles: paperwork and deadlines move things, not emotion
<!-- costs: money=none time=low will=some benefit=small metric=time -->
- Cost: Free. What changes is your expectations and the order of your moves. The first time, you must resist the urge to "argue them into agreement."
- In plain terms: Police, doctors, and clerks are people working to job rules, hemmed in by paperwork, deadlines, and oversight. Whether they're pleasant has almost nothing to do with whether your matter gets done, or in how many days. So collect receipts, note deadlines, appeal through channels — don't try to argue them into agreeing with you.
- Benefit: Swap how you deal with institutions: from "begging, fuming, retelling the grievance" to "collect documents, note deadlines, appeal through channels." What you save is repeated communication and rumination time. This is a one-time mental adjustment, not daily savings. What's checkable are the deadlines themselves, all written in regulations. Public-order (administrative) cases: 30 days from acceptance, extendable by 30 with approval from the next-level public security organ. Where a complainant exists and the case isn't filed, the non-filing notice must be delivered within 3 days; the complainant may request review within 7 days, decided within 30 days; if dissatisfied, 7 more days to petition one level up for re-examination.
- Evidence grade: C
- Sources: 公安部 (2020). 公安机关办理刑事案件程序规定（公安部令第 159 号修正后，第一百七十一、一百七十八、一百七十九条）. <http://www.gov.cn/gongbao/content/2020/content_5549884.htm>；公安部 (2013/2019 修正). 公安机关办理行政案件程序规定（公安部令第 125 号、第 149 号）. <http://www.gov.cn/gongbao/content/2013/content_2321117.htm>
- Note: Why C: "many people treat the person behind the window as a role rather than a worker" is a judgment with no literature to cite. What can be cited is the rules half: deadlines, paperwork, and remedy channels are all in plain text. The numbers here all come from the two ministry regulations also cited in Section 8, item 39 (what happens after you report to police). Following procedure won't fix a bad attitude or guarantee success — it fixes your expectations and your first move. Bank tellers and government windows are the same: their actions are logged, reviewed, and audited. So keep receipts, ask for written confirmation, complain through channels — don't guess at "will they cheat me." Lawful priorities are published: emergency departments triage by severity, not arrival order (Section 24, item 7). To thank a doctor who saved you, use thank-you letters and satisfaction surveys — Section 24, item 11. Wanting to "send something so they try harder" toward law-enforcement is bribery, expressly an aggravating factor — Section 8, item 40. When you meet obvious differential treatment, complain; don't treat it as an unwritten rule to imitate.

### 20. Don't make "how others are doing" your daily reading: cap or kill the apps that feed you peers' updates
<!-- costs: money=none time=low will=yes benefit=large metric=time -->
- Cost: Free. Setting app limits or deactivating takes minutes. The hard part is not reopening them — a long fight against inertia.
- In plain terms: People who habitually compare upward online tend toward worse mood and lower self-esteem. Deactivating Facebook for four weeks slightly raised well-being — and slightly cut current-affairs knowledge. People limiting three social platforms to 10 minutes daily saw loneliness and depression drop in three weeks.
- Benefit: A pooled analysis (meta-analysis): 54 independent samples, 94 effect sizes, N = 36,583, three-level random-effects model. Online upward social comparison correlated with psychological maladjustment at r = 0.330 on average (95% CI 0.289–0.370, the confidence range). By outcome: social-evaluative negative emotions like fear of being looked down on 0.438; anxiety 0.382; depression 0.306; lower well-being 0.268; lower self-esteem 0.263. Age, culture, and collection year didn't change the strength. Longitudinal studies showed no stronger results than cross-sectional ones. A randomized controlled trial with 2,743 US Facebook users: 61% would accept deactivation for under 102 US dollars. Randomized to 4 weeks off versus control. Deactivation freed 60 minutes daily for the average subject. Subjective well-being index rose 0.09 SD — life satisfaction 0.12, anxiety 0.10, depression 0.09, happiness 0.08 — all surviving multiple-comparison correction. The authors offer a benchmark: positive-psychology interventions move well-being about +0.34 SD and depression about −0.23 SD. So this effect is about 25%–40% of a standard psychological intervention. The cost in the same window: current-affairs knowledge down 0.19 SD, political polarization down 0.16 SD. After the experiment, the deactivation group still used Facebook 12 minutes/day less than control (0.31 SD, a 23% reduction). Another RCT: 143 undergraduates randomized to cap Facebook, Instagram, and Snapchat at 10 minutes per platform daily versus use as usual. Three weeks in, the capped group's loneliness and depression fell clearly. Both groups' anxiety and fear of missing out were lower than baseline.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Lei Y, Hu S, Sun Y, Zheng L (2026). "Looking up" linked to feeling down: a meta-analysis of online upward social comparison and psychological maladjustment. Frontiers in Psychology. <https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2026.1825169>；Allcott H, Braghieri L, Eichmeyer S, Gentzkow M (2020). The Welfare Effects of Social Media. American Economic Review, 110(3), 629–676. <https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.20190658>；Allcott H, Braghieri L, Eichmeyer S, Gentzkow M (2019). The Welfare Effects of Social Media. NBER Working Paper No. 25514 (the specific figures here are verified word-for-word against this version; the published article's abstract omits them). <https://www.nber.org/papers/w25514>；Hunt MG, Marx R, Lipson C, Young J (2018). No More FOMO: Limiting Social Media Decreases Loneliness and Depression. Journal of Social and Clinical Psychology, 37(10), 751–768. <https://doi.org/10.1521/jscp.2018.37.10.751>
- Note: The "correlation" half only says two things co-occur — it can't be read as cause; the authors themselves call for more longitudinal work. The randomized half does license causal claims, but the subjects were US Facebook users before the 2018 midterms; carrying it to Chinese platforms is a stretch. Hunt's is 143 undergraduates, and it's the only one. Effect sizes are all modest — don't expect deleting apps to solve mood problems. What to block is the "see how others are doing" class of use; staying in touch with family and friends, looking things up, and job hunting don't count. If you truly can't resist, start by killing push notifications, "people you may know," and the recommendation feed. Total screen-time accounts: Section 4, items 15–16 (hard caps on short video; no scrolling news). What to do when mood sinks: item 18 here. Buying back a sense of identity: Section 6, item 23. Spending more to "move up a notch": Section 6, item 24.

### 21. When you feel "everyone saw me embarrass myself," halve that estimate
<!-- costs: money=none time=low will=some benefit=small metric=time -->
- Cost: Free. Just one extra question when nervous: did they really see it?
- In plain terms: Walk into a room wearing an embarrassing T-shirt, and you'll think nearly half the room can identify who's on it; fewer than a quarter can. Wearing a shirt you think looks good, you'll overestimate sixfold. After conversations, people likewise underestimate how much the other person liked them. So when "everyone will remember my blunder" hits — halve it, and it's still too high.
- Benefit: Experiment 1: participants wore a Barry Manilow T-shirt into a room of others. Wearers estimated 46% of onlookers could correctly name the person on the shirt; actual accuracy was 23% — the estimate exactly double. Subtracting actual from estimated per session: an average overestimate of 23 percentage points (95% CI 9–38, the confidence range); dropping the two sessions with only 2–3 onlookers: 27 points (11–43). Experiment 2: participants chose a T-shirt they considered flattering. Average estimate 48%; actual accuracy 8% — sixfold. Another research program, three settings: stranger dyads, first-year roommates, adult workshops. People systematically underestimated how much conversation partners liked them and enjoyed the conversation — the authors call it the "liking gap." It appeared across conversation lengths and persisted between roommates for months.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Gilovich T, Medvec VH, Savitsky K (2000). The spotlight effect in social judgment: An egocentric bias in estimates of the salience of one's own actions and appearance. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 78(2), 211–222. <https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.78.2.211>；Boothby EJ, Cooney G, Sandstrom GM, Clark MS (2018). The Liking Gap in Conversations: Do People Like Us More Than We Think? Psychological Science, 29(11), 1742–1756. <https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797618783714>
- Note: Why B: all small lab and campus studies, scenarios of T-shirts and stranger chats. Carrying them to "colleagues gossiping" or "misspeaking in a meeting" has no direct evidence. The numbers show the direction and rough multiplier of overestimation — not coefficients to apply. Others' opinions aren't all irrelevant. Where you're genuinely evaluated — interviews, defenses, appraisals, checkup reports — the evaluation is real; prepare for it. What deserves the discount is the everyday "everyone is watching me" feeling; the target there is your own estimation bias.

### 22. Treat "others demand I be perfect" as a symptom, not a fact
<!-- costs: money=none time=low will=some benefit=small metric=mortality -->
- Cost: Free. The hard part is treating the thought as something to manage rather than an accurate measurement of others' actual demands.
- In plain terms: People who constantly feel "everyone around me demands perfection, and falling short means rejection" are more prone to suicidal ideation. "I demand perfection of myself" relates to suicidal ideation far more weakly — about a third as much. After subtracting pre-existing thoughts, the first feeling still tracks later increases in ideation, though modestly.
- Benefit: A pooled analysis (meta-analysis): 45 studies, N = 11,747, undergraduates through psychiatric patients, random-effects model. Weighted average correlations with suicidal ideation: perfectionistic concerns r = .28 (95% CI .24–.32, the confidence range; 42 studies, 7,936 people). Perfectionistic concerns is an umbrella: socially prescribed perfectionism, concern over mistakes, doubts about actions, and the gap between standards and reality. Socially prescribed perfectionism alone: r = .28 (.25–.32; 30 studies, 3,640). Perfectionistic strivings r = .10 (.07–.13; 31 studies, 4,588) — an umbrella of self-oriented perfectionism and personal standards. Parental criticism r = .20 (.11–.29). Versus suicide attempts: perfectionistic concerns r = .12 (.07–.17; 15 studies, 5,275). Socially prescribed perfectionism r = .19 (.08–.29; 8 studies, 689). The longitudinal part: after subtracting baseline suicidal ideation, socially prescribed perfectionism still tracked ideation at follow-up, modestly. Self-oriented and other-oriented perfectionism showed no association.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Smith MM, Sherry SB, Chen S, Saklofske DH, Mushquash C, Flett GL, Hewitt PL (2018). The perniciousness of perfectionism: A meta-analytic review of the perfectionism-suicide relationship. Journal of Personality, 86(3), 522–542. <https://doi.org/10.1111/jopy.12333>
- Note: Why B: most included studies are one-shot questionnaires — no time order. The correlation is moderate (about .28), explaining only a few percent to a tenth of variance. What's measured is ideation and attempts, not deaths — hence "small" on the surrogate-endpoint rule. The authors themselves flag possible publication bias in the concerns-attempts set — publication bias meaning studies with unwelcome results going unpublished. Imputing the missing studies left the direction unchanged. High standards aren't the problem, and there's no need to lower yours. The difference is where the standard comes from, and whether falling short means "I, as a person, am finished." Like the pessimistic forecasts in item 15, it's treatable as a symptom. Suicidal thoughts: call 12356 and move lethal means away — Section 1, item 25. What to do when someone voices them: Section 8, item 15. Depression screening for children: Section 30, item 8.

### 23. When angry, don't vent by smashing things, hitting a bag, or going for a run — slow the body down first
<!-- costs: money=none time=low will=some benefit=medium metric=time -->
- Cost: Free. In the heat, do a few minutes of something that slows your heartbeat: breathe slowly, sit down, be still. The hard part is that venting is exactly what you crave.
- In plain terms: Punching a bag, smashing things, going for a run — arousing forms of "release" — don't lower anger overall. Calming methods like slow breathing, mindfulness, and meditation clearly cut anger and the urge to lash out.
- Benefit: A pooled analysis of 154 studies, 184 samples, 10,189 people. Calming activities — deep breathing, mindfulness, meditation — reduced anger and aggression: effect size g = −0.63, 95% CI −0.82 to −0.43 (the confidence range). Stable across sex, race, age, and culture. Arousing activities — punching bags, jogging, cycling — did nothing overall: g = −0.02, 95% CI −0.13 to 0.09. The authors' conclusion: venting rage and going for a run are not working ways to cool it.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Kjærvik SL, Bushman BJ (2024). A meta-analytic review of anger management activities that increase or decrease arousal: What fuels or douses rage? Clinical Psychology Review. <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cpr.2024.102414>
- Note: What's measured is anger and aggression, not energy; the "time" metric is the author's classification: one less fight is one less mess to clean up. For in-the-moment tools, see Section 22, item 8 (cyclic sighing) and item 17 here (leave the scene). Running isn't useless: long-term it helps low mood — Section 22, item 6 (walk or run when mood is low). It's just not an in-the-moment anger cooler. Don't drive, message, or decide while furious. If someone is hitting you, leave and call the police — then the problem isn't your emotions: Section 8, item 43 (domestic violence).

### 24. With something weighing on you, writing your thoughts and feelings down for several days can help — but don't expect it to cure
<!-- costs: money=none time=low will=no benefit=small metric=time -->
- Cost: Free. The typical study protocol: 3–4 sessions of 15–20 minutes, writing about something distressing — your deepest thoughts and feelings.
- In plain terms: Writing down bad events shows a small average benefit — very small. The pooled conclusions also disagree; two pools found no effect. It costs nearly nothing, so try it. With serious mood problems, don't substitute it for a doctor.
- Benefit: One analysis pooled 146 randomized studies (meta-analysis). People wrote or spoke about personally important matters. The average effect was positive and significant but tiny: effect size r = .075. Another pool admitted only RCTs: 30 trials combined, no significant effect on either physical or psychological health. Sixteen randomized trials in cancer patients and survivors: psychological, physical, and quality-of-life outcomes all nonsignificant — the psychological one g = 0.04, 95% CI −0.06 to 0.14 (the confidence range).
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Frattaroli J (2006). Experimental disclosure and its moderators: a meta-analysis. Psychological Bulletin. <https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.132.6.823>；Mogk C, Otte S, Reinhold-Hurley B, Kröner-Herwig B (2006). Health effects of expressive writing on stressful or traumatic experiences - a meta-analysis. ### 25. Take ibuprofen-type painkillers for menstrual cramps instead of toughing it out; if pain disrupts work or school, or three months of medication doesn't hold it, get a gynecology workup
<!-- costs: money=low time=low will=no benefit=medium metric=time -->
- Cost: A box of ibuprofen runs a dozen-odd yuan, taken only on the painful days. If gynecology is needed, registration and an ultrasound cost some money and half a day.
- In plain terms: For menstrual cramps, ibuprofen-type drugs raise the share with clear pain relief from under 20% to about half — better than acetaminophen, at the price of somewhat more side effects. If pain disrupts work or school, if deep pain during sex, or if three months of medication doesn't control it, get checked for endometriosis. That disease takes 6.7 years on average to diagnose.
- Benefit: A Cochrane review pooled 80 randomized trials, 5,820 women with primary dysmenorrhea — cramps with no other identifiable cause. Versus placebo, NSAIDs relieved pain better (OR 4.37, 95% CI 3.76–5.09, the confidence range). In absolute terms: 18% of placebo takers got moderate-or-better relief versus 45%–53% on NSAIDs. The price: somewhat more side effects (OR 1.29, 1.11–1.51) — 10% on placebo versus 11%–14% on NSAIDs, mostly gastrointestinal (OR 1.58) and nervous-system reactions (OR 2.74). Versus acetaminophen, NSAIDs were also better (OR 1.89, 1.05–3.43), though from only 3 trials. Which NSAID is best: no difference found. The UK NICE guideline (updated 2024) lists situations suggesting endometriosis: chronic pelvic pain; dysmenorrhea affecting daily activities and quality of life; deep pain during or after sex. Also painful bowel movements during periods, blood in urine or painful urination during periods, and infertility with these symptoms. Girls 17 and under are included. The guideline suggests a trial of acetaminophen or NSAIDs first — say, 3 months. If relief is insufficient, proceed to further investigation. Normal gynecological exam and ultrasound don't rule it out. A study across 16 hospitals in 10 countries surveyed 1,418 women undergoing laparoscopy: from symptom onset to surgical diagnosis of endometriosis took 6.7 years on average, mostly lost in primary care. Diagnosed women averaged 10.8 fewer working hours weekly — mostly presenteeism, on the job but less productive. A Dutch online survey of 32,748 women aged 15–45: 13.8% had taken sick leave for menstruation, averaging 1.3 days yearly; 80.7% were at work but less productive during periods — equivalent to 8.9 lost days yearly.
- Evidence grade: A

- Note: Over 60, prior GI bleeding, or on anticoagulants or hormones: ask a doctor first. Details in Section 34, item 3 (ibuprofen-type painkillers).
