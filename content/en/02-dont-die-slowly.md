# 2. Don't die slowly

This section takes only two kinds of chronic risks: those with a large effect on overall mortality, and those with hard evidence. Entries are ranked by value, highest first. A few are marked "randomized trial," meaning people were randomly split into two groups — the most trustworthy kind of result. The other numbers come from studies that only tracked and recorded, without assigning groups. The HRs and RRs such studies produce carry two confounds: these people differed to begin with, and some changed behavior because they were already ill. So those numbers indicate direction and rough size only — they are not a promise that doing the thing lowers your risk by that much.

Entries in this section are grouped by theme, with item numbers in parentheses.

**Tobacco, alcohol, betel nut**: quit smoking, the earlier the better (item 1), no smoking at home or in the car (item 2), get medication first when quitting (item 3), set a quit date (item 4), go to a cessation clinic or call 12320 (item 5), consider e-cigarettes only if you can't quit (item 6), don't chew betel nut (item 8), drink little or none (item 19), if your hands shake when you stop, don't tough it out alone (item 20), count your weekly drinks (item 21).

**Eat and drink less of**: no sugary drinks (item 7), less processed meat (item 18), fewer ultra-processed foods (item 28), no scalding-hot tea or soup (item 30).

**Swap in and eat more of**: switch to low-sodium salt (item 9), a small handful of nuts daily (item 22), swap red meat for fish and poultry (item 23), swap refined grains for whole grains (item 24), tea three or more times a week (item 25), three to four cups of coffee a day (item 26), 5 servings of fruit and vegetables (item 27), chili four or more times a week (item 33), one or two servings of dairy a day (item 34), eggs need not be avoided (item 35).

**Exercise and weight**: walk 7,000–8,000 steps a day (item 11), racket sports (item 14), accumulate bits of vigorous activity (item 15), strength training weekly (item 16), don't sit too long unbroken (item 17), keep BMI at 20–25 (item 32), count the daily dog walk (item 44).

**Sleep and night shifts**: about 7 hours a night (item 13), naps within half an hour (item 37), catch up on sleep the day after a late night (item 38), transfer out of night shifts as early as you can (item 39).

**Kitchen oil and smoke**: don't burn coal or wood for cooking or heating (item 29), don't buy loose machine-pressed peanut oil (item 40), vegetable oil instead of lard and butter (item 41), run the range hood when stir-frying (item 42).

**Teeth, medication, other habits**: brush thoroughly and clean between teeth (item 10), take prescribed medication regularly (item 12), get daylight when out by day (item 31), bathe when you can (item 36), for menopausal hot flashes, get evaluated for hormone therapy (item 43).

### 1. Quit smoking — the earlier the better
<!-- costs: money=none time=low will=yes benefit=large metric=mortality -->
- Cost: Free, and it saves money — a pack a day runs about 20–30 yuan, which stays in your pocket once you quit. The hard part is riding out withdrawal, typically weeks to months.
- In plain terms: Smokers live more than ten years less than non-smokers on average. Quitting before 40 wipes out about 90% of the excess death risk from continuing to smoke. The earlier you quit, the more you get back. And after a bone fracture, keeping smoking makes non-union — the bone failing to knit — about 2.3 times as likely, so don't smoke until the bone has healed.
- Benefit: A US cohort study (record-only, no group assignment) found: current smokers lose more than 10 years of life expectancy versus never-smokers. Quitting before 40 eliminates about 90% of the excess mortality from continued smoking. Quitting at 25–34 recovers about 10 years, at 35–44 about 9 years, at 45–54 about 6 years (reported in life-years). Comparable Chinese studies: in the 2010s, urban male smokers died at 1.65 times the rate of non-smokers (RR 1.65), rural males 1.22. Ten years after quitting voluntarily, that smoking-attributable risk nearly vanishes. Same after fractures: pooled across 19 record-only studies, smokers were about 2.3 times as likely to have fracture non-union (OR 2.32, 95% CI 1.76–3.06, adjusted for other factors). Elevated too for tibial and open fractures. In a Swedish randomized trial, of 50 fracture-surgery patients who joined a 6-week cessation program, 20% had at least one postoperative complication; of 55 who didn't, 38%. For surgery scheduled in advance, quitting can start 4–8 weeks pre-op with weekly coaching and nicotine replacement: postoperative complications fall about 58% (RR 0.42, 95% CI 0.27–0.65, 2 trials, 210 people).
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Jha P et al. (2013). 21st-century hazards of smoking and benefits of cessation in the United States. NEJM. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMsa1211128>；Chen Z et al. (2015). Contrasting male and female trends in tobacco-attributed mortality in China: evidence from successive nationwide prospective cohort studies. Lancet. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(15)00340-2>；Oberg M et al. (2011). Worldwide burden of disease from exposure to second-hand smoke: a retrospective analysis of data from 192 countries. Lancet. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(10)61388-8>（二手烟那两个数字）；Scolaro JA et al. (2014). Cigarette smoking increases complications following fracture: a systematic review. J Bone Joint Surg Am. <https://doi.org/10.2106/JBJS.M.00081>；Nåsell H et al. (2010). Effect of smoking cessation intervention on results of acute fracture surgery: a randomized controlled trial. J Bone Joint Surg Am. <https://doi.org/10.2106/JBJS.I.00627>；Thomsen T et al. (2014). Interventions for preoperative smoking cessation. Cochrane Database Syst Rev. <https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD002294.pub4>（骨折和手术那几个数字）
- Note: Other people's smoke kills too: in 2004, about 603,000 deaths worldwide were attributable to second-hand smoke, roughly 1% of global deaths that year. Even if you don't smoke, avoid others' smoke — and above all keep it away from children. How to quit fills the next four entries: item 3 (cessation medication), item 4 (set a quit date), item 5 (cessation clinic), item 6 (e-cigarettes). The non-union figure comes from record-only studies — smokers may differ from non-smokers in other ways that can't all be adjusted away. The post-fracture cessation trial had only 105 people; under another analysis the difference loses significance.

### 2. Don't smoke at home or in the car — and don't let guests smoke at home
<!-- costs: money=none time=low will=some benefit=large metric=mortality -->
- Cost: Free, no time either. The hard part is speaking up — telling family and guests not to smoke at home.
- In plain terms: About 600,000 people a year die from other people's smoke, nearly three in ten of them children. Long-term second-hand smoke exposure raises hypertension, heart disease, and stroke risk by around 30%. What you breathe at home hurts more than what you breathe outside it.
- Benefit: In 2004, 603,000 deaths worldwide were attributable to second-hand smoke, about 1.0% of global mortality, 28% of them children. Pooling 57 studies: those exposed to second-hand smoke had about 1.28 times the odds of hypertension versus the unexposed (odds ratio). Heart disease 1.39, myocardial infarction 1.50, stroke 1.36. Home exposure carries higher risk than exposure outside the home
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Öberg M, Jaakkola MS, Woodward A, Peruga A, Prüss-Ustün A (2011). Worldwide burden of disease from exposure to second-hand smoke: a retrospective analysis of data from 192 countries. Lancet: "603,000 deaths were attributable to second-hand smoke in 2004, which was about 1·0% of worldwide mortality. 47% of deaths from second-hand smoke occurred in women, 28% in children, and 26% in men", "61% of DALYs were in children". <https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(10)61388-8>；(2026). The Associations Between Secondhand Smoke Exposure and Various Cardiovascular Diseases: A Meta-Analysis. Nicotine & Tobacco Research：57 项研究，"hypertension (OR: 1.28, 95% CI: 1.15 to 1.40), heart disease (OR: 1.39, 95% CI: 1.28 to 1.50), myocardial infarction (OR: 1.50, 95% CI: 1.17 to 1.84), stroke (OR: 1.36, 95% CI: 1.18 to 1.54)" "Home exposure has a higher risk of CVD than non-home exposure". <https://doi.org/10.1093/ntr/ntaf111>
- Note: Start at home: you spend the most time there, and pooled studies show home second-hand smoke is more harmful than exposure elsewhere. Of the health damage from second-hand smoke, 61% lands on children. For quitting yourself, see item 1 in this section.

### 3. Don't quit on willpower alone — get medication first: it more than doubles your odds
<!-- costs: money=low time=low will=some benefit=large metric=mortality -->
- Cost: Nicotine patches and gum are over-the-counter at pharmacy counters. A course runs 8–12 weeks, a few hundred to over a thousand yuan. Varenicline and bupropion are prescription-only, from a cessation clinic or respiratory department. The cost roughly cancels against the cigarettes you're not buying.
- In plain terms: Willpower alone fails most people. Those taking varenicline quit at more than twice the rate of placebo. Nicotine replacement — patches, gum — beats no medication by about half. Patch plus gum together adds another 20-odd percent.
- Benefit: Pooling 41 randomized trials, 17,395 people: the varenicline group's quit rate was 2.32 times placebo's (RR 2.32, 95% CI 2.15–2.51, the confidence interval; high-certainty evidence). Varenicline also beat bupropion (RR 1.36, about 36% higher) and single-form nicotine replacement (RR 1.25, about 25% higher). Nicotine replacement versus nothing: 133 trials, 64,640 people, quit rates about 55% higher (RR 1.55, 95% CI 1.49–1.61). A patch plus a fast-acting form (gum, lozenge) beat a single form by about 27% (RR 1.27, 95% CI 1.17–1.37, 16 trials, 12,169 people, high-certainty evidence).
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Livingstone-Banks J, Fanshawe TR, Thomas KH, et al. (2023). Nicotine receptor partial agonists for smoking cessation. Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, 5, CD006103. <https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD006103.pub8>；Hartmann-Boyce J, Chepkin SC, Ye W, Bullen C, Lancaster T (2018). Nicotine replacement therapy versus control for smoking cessation. Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, 5, CD000146. <https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD000146.pub5>；Theodoulou A, Chepkin SC, Ye W, et al. (2023). Different doses, durations and modes of delivery of nicotine replacement therapy for smoking cessation. Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, 6, CD013308. <https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD013308.pub2>；上海市卫生健康委员会 (2021). 选对药物，让戒烟轻松一点：「市场上可见的戒烟药物主要有三种，即尼古丁替代疗法药物、安非他酮、伐尼克兰」，「尼古丁替代疗法药物属于非处方药（OTC），可通过药店柜台购买；而安非他酮、伐尼克兰属于处方药，须到医院戒烟门诊或呼吸内科就诊，凭医师处方经药师调配后才能得到」. <https://wsjkw.sh.gov.cn/jtyx/20211119/df50681e01ba49f896d54f771d8176ae.html>
- Note: The cessation medications sold in China are exactly those three classes; only nicotine replacement is OTC. Varenicline's common effects are nausea, vivid dreams, poor sleep — anyone with psychiatric history must tell their doctor. Nicotine replacement courses run 8–12 weeks; don't stop abruptly on your own, taper as instructed. Medication only covers the misery of withdrawal weeks, not the situations that trigger wanting one — so combine it with item 4 (set a quit date) and item 5 (cessation clinic).

### 4. Set a quit date and stop dead on it — don't taper first
<!-- costs: money=none time=low will=yes benefit=medium metric=mortality -->
- Cost: Free. Just pick a day on the calendar and tell family and colleagues in advance.
- In plain terms: Many plan to cut down a few cigarettes a day and glide to zero. But at six months, 22% of those who set a date and stopped dead hadn't relapsed, versus 15.5% of the taperers. So pick a day, and stop completely on it.
- Benefit: 697 UK smokers were randomized: one arm stopped abruptly on quit day, the other cut consumption by 75% over the preceding two weeks. Both got nurse behavioral support and nicotine replacement before and after quit day. At week 4, 49.0% of the abrupt group (95% CI 43.8–54.2, the confidence range) hadn't relapsed vs 39.2% (34.0–44.4) of the gradual group, relative risk ratio 0.80 (0.66–0.93). At 6 months: 22.0% (18.0–26.6) vs 15.5% (12.0–19.7), relative risk ratio 0.71 (0.46–0.91). Even among those who preferred tapering, week-4 success was lower than among those preferring abrupt stops (38.3% vs 52.2%).
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Lindson-Hawley N, Banting M, West R, Michie S, Shinkins B, Aveyard P (2016). Gradual Versus Abrupt Smoking Cessation: A Randomized, Controlled Noninferiority Trial. Annals of Internal Medicine, 164(9), 585–592. <https://doi.org/10.7326/M14-2805>；Theodoulou A, Chepkin SC, Ye W, et al. (2023). Different doses, durations and modes of delivery of nicotine replacement therapy for smoking cessation. Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, 6, CD013308（提前用尼古丁替代品那一段）. <https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD013308.pub2>
- Note: Contested. A dedicated Cochrane review once found the two methods roughly equal (RR 0.94, 95% CI 0.79–1.13, 10 trials, 3,760 people), but it was withdrawn in 2019 and no longer updated. So the best current evidence is the randomized trial above, which favors stopping dead. Note also that both arms in that trial started nicotine replacement before quit day — a practice with its own evidence: starting early beats starting on quit day by about 25% (RR 1.25, 95% CI 1.08–1.44, 9 trials, 4,395 people, moderate certainty). So the sensible order is: set the date, patch on two weeks early, stop dead on the day.

### 5. Go to a smoking-cessation clinic, or call 12320 to ask what's available locally
<!-- costs: money=low time=medium will=some benefit=large metric=mortality -->
- Cost: One registration, a few to a few dozen yuan. Behavioral support usually means 4–8 sessions of half an hour to an hour, under 5 hours in total. The hotline is free.
- In plain terms: Medication and "someone checking on you regularly" are two different things, and together they work best. People on medication with a dedicated follow-up person quit at nearly twice the rate of those getting only brief advice. Phone counts too: quitlines that call you back proactively raise success by another 20–30%.
- Benefit: Pooling 52 trials, 19,488 people: medication plus behavioral support quit at 1.83 times the rate of usual care or brief advice (RR 1.83, 95% CI 1.68–1.98, the confidence range; high-quality evidence). In the 43 trials recruited at healthcare settings, 1.97 times (1.79–2.18). Telephone counseling alone: among people who had called a quitline themselves, multiple proactive callbacks beat materials-or-one-call by about 38% (RR 1.38, 95% CI 1.19–1.61, 14 trials, 32,484 people); among those who hadn't called, counselor-initiated calls raised success about 25% (RR 1.25, 95% CI 1.15–1.35, 65 trials, 41,233 people). Both moderate-certainty evidence.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Stead LF, Koilpillai P, Fanshawe TR, Lancaster T (2016). Combined pharmacotherapy and behavioural interventions for smoking cessation. Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, 3, CD008286. <https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD008286.pub3>；Matkin W, Ordóñez-Mena JM, Hartmann-Boyce J (2019). Telephone counselling for smoking cessation. Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, 5, CD002850. <https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD002850.pub4>；中国疾病预防控制中心 (2021). 「中国戒烟平台」微信小程序正式上线：「在健康中国行动控烟行动工作组指导下中国疾病预防控制中心和世界卫生组织驻华代表处联合制作了中国权威戒烟资源库『中国戒烟平台』微信小程序。小程序内容主要包括：戒烟热线、戒烟门诊详细信息，以及线上戒烟服务资源等」；同一站点页脚标注「健康咨询电话：12320」. <https://www.chinacdc.cn/jkyj/yckz/gzdt/202203/t20220310_296389.html>
- Note: Finding it in China: search WeChat for the "China Smoking Cessation Platform" mini-program, which lists local quitlines and cessation clinics; or call 12320 to ask whether your area has a clinic. Most of the trials above were done abroad, and clinic intensity varies widely — treat the numbers as direction only. For the medication to pair with it, see item 3 (cessation medication).

### 6. Consider e-cigarettes only if you've failed to quit; never touch them as a non-smoker
<!-- costs: money=low time=low will=some benefit=medium metric=mortality -->
- Cost: Device plus pods runs a few dozen to a few hundred yuan monthly. Domestically only tobacco flavor is sold, and only at shops holding a tobacco retail license.
- In plain terms: Switching cigarettes for nicotine e-cigarettes beats nicotine patches by about 60% more successful quits — a solid conclusion. But it isn't harmless, and long-term safety data don't exist yet. For a non-smoker, there is nothing to gain and everything to lose.
- Benefit: A continuously updated review included 80 randomized trials, 29,861 people. Nicotine e-cigarettes versus nicotine replacement therapy: quit success about 61% higher (RR 1.61, 95% CI 1.23–2.12, the confidence range; high-certainty evidence, 11 trials, 4,114 people). In absolute terms, roughly 4 extra quitters per 100 (1 to 7 more). Serious adverse events were broadly similar between groups (risk difference 0.01, 95% CI −0.01 to 0.02, moderate certainty). Versus behavioral support alone or none: about 75% higher (RR 1.75, 95% CI 1.39–2.20, low-certainty evidence).
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Lindson N, Livingstone-Banks J, Butler AR, et al. (2026). Electronic cigarettes for smoking cessation. Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, 8, CD010216. <https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD010216.pub11>；国家烟草专卖局 (2022). 电子烟管理办法（国家烟草专卖局公告 2022 年第 1 号，2022 年 5 月 1 日施行）第十八条：从事电子烟零售业务，要向烟草专卖行政主管部门申领烟草专卖零售许可证。第二十二条：「禁止向未成年人出售电子烟产品。」第二十三条第二款：「任何个人、法人或者其他组织不得通过本办法规定的电子烟交易管理平台以外的信息网络销售电子烟产品、雾化物和电子烟用烟碱等。」第二十六条：「禁止销售除烟草口味外的调味电子烟和可自行添加雾化物的电子烟。」. <http://www.gov.cn/gongbao/content/2022/content_5697988.htm>
- Note: Contested. WHO does not recommend e-cigarettes as a cessation aid, citing insufficient long-term safety data and their role in starting young people on nicotine. The UK puts them in cessation services. China hasn't banned e-cigarettes outright but regulates them tightly under the E-cigarette Administration Measures: only tobacco flavor may be sold — fruit flavors and refillable devices are out; only licensed physical shops may sell; selling via any website, online shop, or social app outside the official national e-cigarette trading platform is illegal; sales to minors prohibited. So fruit-flavored vapes still on sale come through illegal channels outside regulation — that's how "high" vapes spiked with synthetic cannabinoids circulate; see Section 22, item 4 (don't accept pods offered by others). The review tested regulated nicotine products only; mystery-market vape oil isn't covered. Order of attack: try item 3 first (cessation medication) — harder evidence, cheaper too.

### 7. No sugary drinks — and switching to the sugar-free kind isn't a solution either
<!-- costs: money=none time=low will=some benefit=large metric=mortality -->
- Cost: Free, no time either. Switching to plain water or unsweetened tea saves money. The hard part is breaking the sweet-drink habit, sugar-free versions included.
- In plain terms: People drinking two or more sweetened bottles a day are about 20% more likely to die over the same period than people who almost never do. One to two a day still adds 10-odd percent. And switching to diet doesn't show a benefit: people drinking two or more artificially sweetened drinks a day run about a quarter higher.
- Benefit: Two large US cohort studies — record-only, no assignment — about 118,000 people and 36,000 deaths. Those drinking 2+ servings daily had about 21% higher mortality than those drinking under 1 a month (HR 1.21). One to two servings daily: about 14% higher (HR 1.14). The European EPIC cohort, also record-only, 450,000 people across 10 countries. Two-plus glasses daily versus under one a month: sugar-sweetened soft drinks about 8% higher (HR 1.08, 95% CI 1.01–1.16). Artificially sweetened (diet) drinks about 26% higher (HR 1.26, 1.16–1.35). All soft drinks pooled: about 17% higher (HR 1.17, 1.11–1.22).
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Malik VS et al. (2019). Long-Term Consumption of Sugar-Sweetened and Artificially Sweetened Beverages and Risk of Mortality in US Adults. Circulation. <https://doi.org/10.1161/CIRCULATIONAHA.118.037401>；Mullee A, Romaguera D, Pearson-Stuttard J, et al. (2019). Association Between Soft Drink Consumption and Mortality in 10 European Countries. JAMA Internal Medicine, 179(11), 1479-1490. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2019.2478>
- Note: The dispute is over diet drinks. The association for artificially sweetened drinks is stronger than for sugary ones — most likely reverse causation: people already overweight or diabetic are the ones who switch to diet. So don't read this as "sweeteners are more toxic." It does show, though, that "switch to diet and you're fine" has no evidential support. Plain water or unsweetened tea is safest. Also, these studies only track and record — heavy soda drinkers have worse lifestyles overall, so the numbers may be inflated.

### 8. Don't chew betel nut
<!-- costs: money=none time=low will=some benefit=large metric=mortality -->
- Cost: Free, no time, and you save the money spent on betel. The hard part is quitting the chewing habit.
- In plain terms: Betel chewers are about 20% more likely to die over the same period than non-chewers. Diabetes and metabolic syndrome risk run nearly 50% higher. Oral and esophageal cancer risk is on top of that.
- Benefit: Pooling 17 Asian studies, 388,000 people. Betel chewers had about 21% higher mortality than non-chewers (relative risk 1.21, P=0.02). The mortality figure counted 179,600 of them. Diabetes risk about 47% higher (1.47). Metabolic syndrome about 51% higher (1.51). Betel nut is itself a known risk factor for oral and esophageal cancer
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Yamada T, Hara K, Kadowaki T (2013). Chewing betel quid and the risk of metabolic disease, cardiovascular disease, and all-cause mortality: a meta-analysis. PLoS One, 8(8), e70679. <https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0070679>
- Note: The longer you've chewed and the more per day, the higher the oral cancer risk. After quitting, risk drifts down over time. Betel containing tobacco is worse still

### 9. Swap the household salt for low-sodium (potassium) salt
<!-- costs: money=low time=low will=no benefit=medium metric=mortality -->
- Cost: A bag costs a few yuan more than regular salt. Swap it while shopping — no extra time. Taste barely changes.
- In plain terms: For people who've had a stroke, or over-60s with high blood pressure, switching the household salt to low-sodium salt cuts the 5-year risk of death by about 12% and of stroke by about 14%.
- Benefit: A randomized trial in rural China, 20,995 people — all either stroke survivors or hypertensives over 60 — followed 4.74 years. The low-sodium-salt group versus regular salt: death risk about 12% lower (RR 0.88). Stroke about 14% lower (RR 0.86). Major cardiovascular events about 13% lower (RR 0.87). Hyperkalemia events showed no statistical difference between groups.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Neal B et al. (2021). Effect of Salt Substitution on Cardiovascular Events and Death. NEJM. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa2105675>；O'Donnell M et al. (2014). Urinary sodium and potassium excretion, mortality, and cardiovascular events. NEJM. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1311889>（争议方 PURE）
- Note: Contested. PURE is a long-running observational study, no assignment. It reported: people excreting under 3 g sodium a day had about 27% higher risk of death plus cardiovascular events; over 7 g a day, about 15% higher. On its numbers, both too little and too much are bad, with the middle best. But in the rural-China randomized trial cited here, low-sodium salt only replaced part of the intake — it doesn't push sodium that low. Note also that the trial subjects were high-risk older people; healthy young people gain much less from switching. Anyone with renal insufficiency, or on potassium-sparing drugs, should ask a doctor before switching.

### 10. Brush thoroughly, clean between your teeth daily, replace missing teeth promptly
<!-- costs: money=low time=low will=some benefit=medium metric=mortality -->
- Cost: Floss or interdental brushes run a few dozen yuan a year; a cleaning, one to two hundred each. Two or three extra minutes a day. The hard part is making interdental cleaning a daily habit. If a manual brush doesn't get you clean, an electric one costs a few hundred yuan, with heads to replace periodically.
- In plain terms: People who clean between their teeth daily with floss or interdental brushes are about 10% less likely to die over the same period. Elders who've lost all their teeth run about 90% higher mortality. Those with fewer than 20 teeth, about double. Electric brushes remove 10–20% more plaque than manual and reduce gum inflammation somewhat — though how much that matters is unclear.
- Benefit: A Japanese study, 9,676 people, 6 years of follow-up. Users of interdental cleaning tools had about 11% lower mortality (hazard ratio 0.89). Users of tongue cleaners, about 23% lower (0.77). Separately, pooled studies of community-dwelling elders: those with no teeth at all had about 1.87 times the odds of death (odds ratio, 95% CI 1.35–2.59, the confidence range). Fewer than 20 teeth: about 2.04 times (1.67–2.49). Electric versus manual: pooling 51 randomized trials, over 1–3 months electric brushing left about 11% less plaque, over 3 months about 21% less. Gingivitis down about 6% and 11% respectively. The review authors say how much these differences matter for health remains unclear
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Wang K, Matsuyama Y, Kiuchi S, et al. (2026). Routine oral health practices and all-cause mortality. Journal of Dentistry. <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jdent.2026.106789>；Ko MJ, Seo S, So JS, et al. (2026). Deteriorated oral health and function as risk factors for physical disability and mortality in community-dwelling older adults: a systematic review and meta-analysis. European Geriatric Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.1007/s41999-025-01319-4>；Yaacob M, Worthington HV, Deacon SA, et al. (2014). Powered versus manual toothbrushing for oral health. Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews. <https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD002281.pub3>；Deacon SA, Glenny AM, Deery C, et al. (2010). Different powered toothbrushes for plaque control and gingival health. Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews. <https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD004971.pub2>
- Note: Contested. These studies only track and record. People with bad teeth tend to have worse overall health and finances — whether the teeth drag down the body or the body drags down the teeth can't be disentangled. But the cost is minimal, and periodontitis and missing teeth directly impair eating. Among electric brushes, the round oscillating-rotating heads have the most trials and the clearest edge over manual. Which electric type is best between themselves — the independent reviews declined to say, too few trials. For what an implant costs and which dental procedures insurance covers, see Section 24, item 12 (ask the all-inclusive price before an implant)

### 11. Walk 7,000–8,000 steps a day, or accumulate 150–300 minutes of brisk walking a week
<!-- costs: money=none time=high will=some benefit=large metric=mortality -->
- Cost: Free. By step count, that's 60–90 minutes of walking a day, splittable across commutes and errands. By exercise time, 20–45 minutes a day. The hard part is keeping it up for years.
- In plain terms: People walking about 7,800 steps a day are roughly 45% less likely to die over the same period than those walking 3,500. By time it's the same story: 150–300 minutes of brisk walking a week beats no exercise by about 30%. Past a certain amount the curve flattens — over 60, 6,000–8,000 steps is enough. Meet either measure and you're covered.
- Benefit: Pooling 15 cohort studies — record-only, no assignment — 47,471 people, 3,013 deaths. Quartiles by daily steps; the lowest quartile's median was 3,553 steps/day. The other three medians: 5,801, 7,842, 10,901 steps/day. Their mortality risks ran about 40%, 45%, 53% lower than the lowest (HR 0.60, 0.55, 0.47). For the over-60s, benefits flatten after 6,000–8,000 steps; under 60, after 8,000–10,000. Another pooled analysis: benefit starts from about 3,867 steps/day, and each additional 1,000 steps/day lowers mortality about 15%. By exercise time: pooled cohort studies (record-only) — people at 1–2 times the guideline minimum had about 31% lower mortality than the inactive (HR 0.69). That's 7.5–15 MET·h/week, roughly brisk walking 150–300 min/week. Below the minimum still meant about 20% lower (HR 0.80). At 3–5 times the minimum, about 39% lower (HR 0.61) — the peak. Beyond that, no further drop, but no harm either (10+ times: HR 0.69). Another accelerometer-based pooled study: the most-active quarter had about 48% lower mortality than the least (HR 0.52). An accelerometer is a wearable device that measures movement directly.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Paluch AE et al. (2022). Daily steps and all-cause mortality: a meta-analysis of 15 international cohorts. Lancet Public Health. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S2468-2667(21)00302-9>；Banach M et al. (2023). The association between daily step count and all-cause and cardiovascular mortality: a meta-analysis. European Journal of Preventive Cardiology. <https://doi.org/10.1093/eurjpc/zwad229>；Arem H et al. (2015). Leisure time physical activity and mortality: a detailed pooled analysis of the dose-response relationship. JAMA Internal Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2015.0533>；Ekelund U et al. (2019). Dose-response associations between accelerometry measured physical activity and sedentary time and all cause mortality: systematic review and harmonised meta-analysis. BMJ. <https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.l4570>
- Note: Step count and exercise time measure the same thing in different units — meet either one. Going from 4,000 to 7,000 steps is the best-value stretch; no need to force 10,000. Remember these studies only track: among the least active are people already too sick to move — they moved less because they were failing, not the reverse. So the computed gaps run large. The device-measured studies have short follow-up and older participants; the real benefit isn't "risk down to 0.52 times." But the more-you-move-the-better direction is clear.

### 12. With hypertension or high cholesterol, take your medication as prescribed — don't stop on your own
<!-- costs: money=low time=low will=some benefit=large metric=mortality -->
- Cost: Volume-procured antihypertensives and statins run a few to a few dozen yuan a month. Once a day, barely any time. The hard part is taking them indefinitely, not stopping when you feel fine.
- In plain terms: Every 10 mmHg blood pressure comes down, death probability drops about 13% and stroke about 27%. Every 1 unit statins lower LDL cholesterol, death probability drops about 10%. People who take their pills on schedule run 30%–45% lower mortality than frequent missers.
- Benefit: Pooled randomized trials: every 10 mmHg systolic reduction lowers death risk about 13% (RR 0.87). Systolic is the higher blood-pressure number. Major cardiovascular events down about 20% (RR 0.80). Stroke down about 27% (RR 0.73). Heart failure down about 28% (RR 0.72). Statins lower LDL cholesterol: every 1.0 mmol/L drop lowers death risk about 10% (RR 0.90); the same drop lowers major vascular events about 22% (RR 0.78). Separately, pooled cohort studies (record-only): those taking ≥80% as prescribed had lower mortality than frequent missers — statins about 45% lower (RR 0.55), antihypertensives about 29% lower (RR 0.71).
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Ettehad D et al. (2016). Blood pressure lowering for prevention of cardiovascular disease and death: a systematic review and meta-analysis. Lancet. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(15)01225-8>；Cholesterol Treatment Trialists' (CTT) Collaboration (2010). Efficacy and safety of more intensive lowering of LDL cholesterol: a meta-analysis of data from 170 000 participants in 26 randomised trials. Lancet. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(10)61350-5>；Chowdhury R et al. (2013). Adherence to cardiovascular therapy: a meta-analysis of prevalence and clinical consequences. European Heart Journal. <https://doi.org/10.1093/eurheartj/eht295>
- Note: This applies only to people a doctor has judged need medication; healthy people need none. The "adherence" numbers come from tracking — people who adhere are also more self-disciplined overall, which inflates the benefit. The randomized-trial numbers above are more trustworthy. This section has no separate glucose-control entry: the mortality benefit of pushing glucose lower isn't as consistent as for blood pressure and lipids.

### 13. Sleep about 7 hours a night, on a fixed schedule
<!-- costs: money=none time=high will=some benefit=medium metric=mortality -->
- Cost: Free. It takes time — for most people, trading phone-scrolling for sleep. The hard part is fixing your bedtime and wake time.
- In plain terms: People chronically sleeping under 7 hours run about 10% higher mortality over the same period. The more erratic the schedule, the higher the risk. The most regular sleepers run 20% to nearly 50% lower than the least regular. In other words: how regular your sleep is predicts death better than how long it is.
- Benefit: Pooling 16 cohort studies — record-only — 1.38 million people, 113,000 deaths. Short sleepers ran about 12% higher mortality (RR 1.12); long sleepers about 30% higher (RR 1.30). Another pooled analysis by duration band: the 7-hour band had the lowest risk. Below 7 hours, each hour less added about 6% (RR 1.06). Above 7, each hour more added about 13% (RR 1.13). The UK Biobank has wristwatch records for about 61,000 people — record-only. Splitting them into five bands by sleep regularity, the four more-regular bands ran 20%–48% lower mortality than the least regular. Sleep regularity predicts death better than sleep duration. Another UK Biobank study measured sleep timing by wristwatch in 51,562 people, with 3,853 incident cardiovascular diseases. Social jet lag means the gap between weekday and weekend sleep midpoints. Those with 2+ hours of social jet lag ran about 30% higher cardiovascular risk (HR 1.30, 95% CI 1.11–1.54). The association held even among people with normal sleep duration.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Cappuccio FP et al. (2010). Sleep duration and all-cause mortality: a systematic review and meta-analysis of prospective studies. Sleep. <https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/33.5.585>；Yin J et al. (2017). Relationship of Sleep Duration With All-Cause Mortality and Cardiovascular Events: A Systematic Review and Dose-Response Meta-Analysis of Prospective Cohort Studies. JAHA. <https://doi.org/10.1161/JAHA.117.005947>；Windred DP et al. (2024). Sleep regularity is a stronger predictor of mortality risk than sleep duration: A prospective cohort study. Sleep. <https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/zsad253>；Kumar N, Krishnamurthy S (2026). Social jet lag is associated with incident cardiovascular disease independent of sleep duration and cardiac genetic risk. Journal of Internal Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.1111/joim.70133>
- Note: What to fix is sleeping too little and sleeping erratically — don't deliberately compress sleep. The high risk among long sleepers is mostly reverse causation: depression, chronic illness, and sleep apnea make people sleep more. Also, the regularity finding currently rests on two cohort studies, record-only — on its own that's Grade B evidence. For recovering after a late night, see item 38 (catch up the very next night).

### 14. Play racket sports — tennis, badminton, table tennis
<!-- costs: money=low time=medium will=some benefit=large metric=mortality -->
- Cost: Court fees run a few dozen yuan a session. About 2 hours a week. The hard part is rounding up people and a court, every week.
- In plain terms: Regular racket-sport players run about half the mortality of non-players over the same period, and 56% lower cardiovascular death. Swimmers run about 28% lower. Running and football showed no difference in this dataset.
- Benefit: A UK cohort of 80,306 comparing participants in a sport with non-participants in it. Racket-sport players (tennis, badminton, table tennis) had about 47% lower mortality (hazard ratio 0.53, 95% CI 0.40–0.69, the confidence range). Cardiovascular death about 56% lower (0.44, 0.24–0.83). Swimmers: 0.72 and 0.59 respectively; aerobics 0.73 and 0.64; cycling 0.85. Running and football showed no statistical difference
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Oja P, Kelly P, Pedisic Z, et al. (2017). Associations of specific types of sports and exercise with all-cause and cardiovascular-disease mortality: a cohort study of 80 306 British adults. British Journal of Sports Medicine, 51(10), 812-817. <https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2016-096822>
- Note: Contested. These studies only track. People who play sports are healthier to begin with, and more social. That runners showed no difference here also hints that "what kind of person picks what sport" is doing some of the work. Don't conclude running is useless from this entry — this section has entries that count total exercise volume

### 15. Accumulate bits of vigorous effort — stairs, hurrying on foot — to 4–5 minutes a day
<!-- costs: money=none time=low will=some benefit=large metric=mortality -->
- Cost: Free, and no extra workout time to carve out. The hard part is remembering to take the stairs and walk faster rather than saving effort.
- In plain terms: For people who do no dedicated exercise at all, just having 3 one-to-two-minute bursts of vigorous effort a day — think stair climbs, hurrying somewhere on foot — runs about 40% lower mortality than none, and nearly 50% lower cardiovascular death.
- Benefit: A UK Biobank study followed 25,200 people who did no dedicated exercise, average 6.9 years, 852 deaths. Some had 3 daily bursts of 1–2 minutes of incidental vigorous activity. Versus none, their all-cause and cancer mortality ran 38%–40% lower, cardiovascular death 48%–49% lower. Those accumulating 4.4 minutes of such activity daily ran 26%–30% lower all-cause and cancer mortality, and 32%–34% lower cardiovascular death
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Stamatakis E, Ahmadi MN, Gill JMR, et al. (2022). Association of wearable device-measured vigorous intermittent lifestyle physical activity with mortality. Nature Medicine, 28, 2521-2529. <https://doi.org/10.1038/s41591-022-02100-x>
- Note: Contested. This study measured activity with wearables — more reliable than questionnaires. But it is still record-only, no trial, and just 6.9 years of follow-up. The subjects were non-exercisers; people already exercising regularly don't need this entry

### 16. Do 30–60 minutes of strength training a week
<!-- costs: money=none time=medium will=some benefit=medium metric=mortality -->
- Cost: Bodyweight squats and push-ups are free. 1–2 sessions a week, 20–30 minutes each. The hard part is putting it in the weekly schedule.
- In plain terms: People training strength 30–60 minutes a week run 10–20% lower mortality than non-trainers over the same period. More doesn't help further. Strength plus aerobic together works best.
- Benefit: Pooled cohort studies (record-only): strength trainers run 10%–17% lower mortality than non-trainers. More isn't better — the largest reduction, about 10%–20%, sits at roughly 30–60 minutes a week, with no extra benefit beyond. Those doing both strength and aerobic run lower mortality than those doing neither.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Momma H et al. (2022). Muscle-strengthening activities are associated with lower risk and mortality in major non-communicable diseases: a systematic review and meta-analysis of cohort studies. British Journal of Sports Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2021-105061>
- Note: These studies only track, and exercise volume was self-reported. The evidence for "overtraining turns harmful" is weak — don't restrict your volume on that account. Strength training also protects older people against falls and muscle loss; that's in Section 1.

### 17. Don't sit too long unbroken — get up and move at intervals
<!-- costs: money=none time=low will=some benefit=large metric=mortality -->
- Cost: Free; standing up a few times costs little time. The hard part is losing track once seated — set a reminder.
- In plain terms: The longest sitters run 2.6 times the mortality of the shortest over the same period. Long unbroken sitting spells add a separate risk. But 60–75 minutes of moderate activity a day essentially cancels the extra sitting risk.
- Benefit: A US cohort, record-only, measured with accelerometers. Splitting people into quartiles by total daily sitting: the top quartile ran 2.63 times the mortality of the bottom (HR 2.63). Splitting instead by longest unbroken bout: the top quartile ran 1.96 times the bottom (HR 1.96). So total sitting and bout length each associate independently. A separate pooled analysis of over a million people, record-only: those sitting 8+ hours a day with almost no exercise ran 1.59 times the mortality (HR 1.59) of those sitting under 4 hours and most active. About 60–75 minutes of moderate activity daily offsets the excess sitting risk — even the most active sitting 8+ hours showed HR 1.04, no statistical difference.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Diaz KM et al. (2017). Patterns of Sedentary Behavior and Mortality in U.S. Middle-Aged and Older Adults: A National Cohort Study. Annals of Internal Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.7326/M17-0212>；Ekelund U et al. (2016). Does physical activity attenuate, or even eliminate, the detrimental association of sitting time with mortality? A harmonised meta-analysis of data from more than 1 million men and women. Lancet. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(16)30370-1>
- Note: The solid conclusion: how much you move matters more than how long you sit; with enough exercise, sitting risk largely disappears. Remember these studies only track — the longest sitters include many already ill, so "risk up to 2.63×" clearly contains some "sick first, sat more." Also, in the same pool, 3+ hours of daily TV carried risk at every activity level; the most active just pushed the threshold to 5 hours.

### 18. Eat less processed meat (ham, bacon, sausage, luncheon meat)
<!-- costs: money=none time=low will=some benefit=large metric=mortality -->
- Cost: Free — even saves money — and takes no time. The hard part is cutting back a favorite.
- In plain terms: The heaviest eaters of ham, bacon, and sausage run about 20% higher mortality than the lightest over the same period. Each extra daily serving of processed meat adds about that much again.
- Benefit: Pooled cohort studies (record-only): the highest processed-meat group ran about 23% higher mortality than the lowest (RR 1.23). Total red meat: about 29% higher (RR 1.29). Unprocessed red meat: about 10% higher (RR 1.10), but that one showed no statistical difference. Another pool computed per daily amount: each extra serving of processed meat daily, about 23% higher mortality (RR 1.23); each extra serving of red meat, about 10% (RR 1.10).
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Larsson SC, Orsini N (2014). Red meat and processed meat consumption and all-cause mortality: a meta-analysis. American Journal of Epidemiology. <https://doi.org/10.1093/aje/kwt261>；Schwingshackl L et al. (2017). Food groups and risk of all-cause mortality: a systematic review and meta-analysis of prospective studies. American Journal of Clinical Nutrition. <https://doi.org/10.3945/ajcn.117.153148>；Johnston BC et al. (2019). Unprocessed Red Meat and Processed Meat Consumption: Dietary Guideline Recommendations From the NutriRECS Consortium. Annals of Internal Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.7326/M19-1621>（争议方 NutriRECS 指南）
- Note: Contested. The NutriRECS guideline, using the standard GRADE scoring, rated these studies as less than reliable, and offered only a weak suggestion to keep eating as you do. The dispute is over how hard the evidence is, not the direction. No study finds processed meat beneficial. Unprocessed red meat's effect is smaller and statistically indistinct. The thing to rein in is processed meat.

### 19. Drink little, or not at all
<!-- costs: money=none time=low will=some benefit=large metric=mortality -->
- Cost: Free — saves money — and takes no time. The hard part is a moment of awkwardness declining at social occasions.
- In plain terms: Past 100 g of pure alcohol a week, the more you drink, the earlier you die. 100 g of pure alcohol is about 2.5 L of beer. At 40, drinking 100–200 g a week costs roughly half a year of life; 200–350 g, one to two years; over 350 g, four to five years. Counting overall health, the best-value amount is none.
- Benefit: Pooling 83 prospective studies, record-only, 600,000 current drinkers. Mortality's lowest point sits at or below 100 g pure alcohol weekly — roughly 2.5 L of 5% beer, or 300 mL of 40% spirits. At age 40, 100–200 g weekly costs about 6 months of life expectancy; 200–350 g, 1–2 years; over 350 g, 4–5 years (reported in life-years). The Global Burden of Disease study GBD 2016, pooling all health damage, put the lowest-risk drinking level at 0 drinks/week. And one pool corrected for design biases: versus lifetime abstainers, 1.3–24 g daily ran about 7% lower mortality (RR 0.93) — no statistical difference. 45–64 g daily ran about 19% higher (RR 1.19); 65 g and up, about 35% higher (RR 1.35).
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Wood AM et al. (2018). Risk thresholds for alcohol consumption: combined analysis of individual-participant data for 599 912 current drinkers in 83 prospective studies. Lancet. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(18)30134-X>；GBD 2016 Alcohol Collaborators (2018). Alcohol use and burden for 195 countries and territories, 1990–2016: a systematic analysis for the Global Burden of Disease Study 2016. Lancet. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(18)31310-2>；Zhao J et al. (2023). Association Between Daily Alcohol Intake and Risk of All-Cause Mortality: A Systematic Review and Meta-analyses. JAMA Network Open. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2023.6185>；Di Castelnuovo A et al. (2006). Alcohol dosing and total mortality in men and women: an updated meta-analysis of 34 prospective studies. Archives of Internal Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.1001/archinte.166.22.2437>（争议方）
- Note: Contested. The "a little is good" side is a pool of 34 long cohorts reporting up to 17%–18% lower total mortality among light drinkers, with limits of 4 drinks daily for men and 2 for women, below which heavier drinkers died less. The other side argues the "abstainer" group mixes in ex-drinkers who quit for illness and the constitutionally frail. A pool that specifically removed those confounds found the protection vanished once they were gone. The GBD also puts zero as safest. The safe reading: light drinking won't visibly shorten life; heavy drinking certainly will. "Start drinking for your health" has no basis. For cutting down, see item 21 (count first, then talk to a doctor). Daily drinkers who shake when they stop must not quit cold: item 20.

### 20. Daily drinkers whose hands shake and heart races when they stop: don't tough it out alone
<!-- costs: money=low time=medium will=some benefit=large metric=mortality -->
- Cost: One psychiatry or addiction-medicine registration, a few to a few dozen yuan. Severe cases need a few days in hospital.
- In plain terms: Heavy daily drinkers who stop abruptly get withdrawal. Mild: tremor, sweating, palpitations, insomnia. Severe: seizures or delirium tremens — a medical emergency that kills. Norway followed over 30,000 people; among those who'd had delirium tremens, 8% died each year afterward. If you want to quit, go to a hospital first and say exactly how much you drink daily.
- Benefit: A 2026 clinical review states plainly: after abrupt cessation or major reduction, long-term heavy drinkers develop withdrawal syndrome — tremor, autonomic hyperactivity, anxiety, insomnia, perceptual disturbances, seizures, and delirium tremens — "common and potentially life-threatening" in ordinary medical practice. The Norwegian national register cohort covered 36,287 people diagnosed 2009–2015 with alcohol dependence, withdrawal state, or delirium tremens: annual mortality 8.0% in the delirium tremens group, 5.0% in withdrawal state, 3.6% in dependence. The delirium tremens group's standardized mortality ratio was 9.8 (95% CI 8.9–10.7, the confidence range) — about 9.8 times the death rate of age-matched peers.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Caputo F, Lungaro L, Costanzini A, De Giorgio R, Addolorato G (2026). Alcohol withdrawal syndrome in hospitalized patients: a practical review. European Journal of Internal Medicine, 107103. <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ejim.2026.107103>；Bramness JG, Heiberg IH, Høye A, Rossow I (2023). Mortality and alcohol-related morbidity in patients with delirium tremens, alcohol withdrawal state or alcohol dependence in Norway: A register-based prospective cohort study. Addiction, 118(12), 2352–2359. <https://doi.org/10.1111/add.16297>
- Note: Self-check: drinking nearly every day; a day off brings tremor, sweat, racing heart, insomnia; needing a morning drink to feel right — any one of these means don't stop alone. The hospital routine is benzodiazepines to carry you through the days, plus thiamine (vitamin B1). Grade B because the Norwegian cohort shows what happens after delirium tremens, not what happens after quitting cold — the connection is inference. The quitting still needs doing; just do it in a hospital. For what counts as heavy: item 19. For cutting down: item 21.

### 21. To drink less: first count a week's worth, then talk with a doctor for a few minutes
<!-- costs: money=none time=low will=some benefit=small metric=mortality -->
- Cost: Free. Counting drinks is a daily note. Asking at a clinic visit costs nothing extra.
- In plain terms: A doctor spending a few minutes reviewing your drinking, spelling out the risks, and setting a goal gets you, a year later, 20 g less pure alcohol a week on average — about half a liter of beer. Longer talks add nothing. For those who can't stop, two drugs proven abroad help: roughly 1 in 12 treated no longer returns to heavy drinking.
- Benefit: Pooling 34 randomized trials, 15,197 people: brief-intervention recipients drank 20 g less pure alcohol per week a year later than minimal-or-no-intervention controls (95% CI 12 to 28 g less, the confidence range; moderate-quality evidence). Baseline intake in these studies averaged 244 g weekly. Brief intervention means advice or lifestyle counseling in no more than 5 sessions totaling under 60 minutes; longer talks added nothing. Drugs: pooling 122 randomized trials, 22,803 people — acamprosate: for every 12 treated (95% CI 8 to 26), 1 extra person returned to no drinking at all; oral naltrexone 50 mg daily: for every 12 treated (8 to 26), 1 extra person avoided returning to heavy drinking.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Kaner EF, Beyer FR, Muirhead C, et al. (2018). Effectiveness of brief alcohol interventions in primary care populations. Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, 2, CD004148. <https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD004148.pub4>；Jonas DE, Amick HR, Feltner C, et al. (2014). Pharmacotherapy for adults with alcohol use disorders in outpatient settings: a systematic review and meta-analysis. JAMA, 311(18), 1889–1900. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2014.3628>
- Note: Benefit is "small" because both lines of evidence measure drinking amounts, not mortality — surrogate endpoints only, by this book's rule. The drug trials were mostly abroad, subjects mostly already meeting dependence criteria, all with psychosocial support bundled in — the numbers are the extra benefit on top. Whether these two drugs are indicated and prescribable in China follows the doctor and the label; don't self-order online. If withdrawal has already appeared, see item 20 (don't quit cold).

### 22. Eat a small handful of nuts a day
<!-- costs: money=low time=low will=no benefit=large metric=mortality -->
- Cost: 28 g a day runs about 200–300 yuan a year. Eaten on the go, no time.
- In plain terms: Regular nut eaters run about 20% lower mortality than non-eaters over the same period. Once a week already shows a difference — about 10% lower. A handful a day is where it's most visible.
- Benefit: Two US cohort studies, 119,000 people, over 3 million person-years. A person-year is people multiplied by years followed each. Versus non-eaters: under once weekly, about 7% lower mortality (hazard ratio 0.93, 95% CI 0.90–0.96, the confidence range); once weekly, about 11% (0.89, 0.86–0.93); 2–4 times, about 13% (0.87, 0.83–0.90); 5–6 times, about 15% (0.85, 0.79–0.91); 7+ times, about 20% (0.80, 0.73–0.86)
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Bao Y, Han J, Hu FB, et al. (2013). Association of nut consumption with total and cause-specific mortality. New England Journal of Medicine, 369(21), 2001-2011. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1307352>
- Note: Buy plain, unsalted. Nuts aren't low-calorie — don't graze on them freely. Remember these studies only follow people; nut eaters have better lifestyles overall, so discount the numbers

### 23. Swap part of your red meat for fish and poultry
<!-- costs: money=none time=low will=some benefit=small metric=mortality -->
- Cost: Free, no time. It's swapping one meat for another, not buying more. The hard part is shifting taste habits.
- In plain terms: Two extra weekly servings of processed or red meat raise mortality about 3% over the same period. Swap the same servings for fish or poultry and the increase disappears. The effect here is small for this section — do it in passing, don't agonize.
- Benefit: Pooling 6 US cohort studies, nearly 30,000 people. Two extra weekly servings of processed meat: mortality about 3% higher (hazard ratio 1.03, 95% CI 1.02–1.05, the confidence range). Two extra of unprocessed red meat: also about 3% (1.03, 1.01–1.05). The same servings swapped to poultry: 0.99 (0.97–1.02); to fish: 0.99 (0.97–1.01). Neither difference was statistically significant
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Zhong VW, Van Horn L, Greenland P, et al. (2020). Associations of Processed Meat, Unprocessed Red Meat, Poultry, or Fish Intake With Incident Cardiovascular Disease and All-Cause Mortality. JAMA Internal Medicine, 180(4), 503-512. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2019.6969>
- Note: Two weekly servings move the needle very little — don't expect meat-swapping to buy years. But if you're spending the money anyway, fish and poultry are the better picks

### 24. Swap part of your refined grains for whole grains
<!-- costs: money=low time=low will=some benefit=large metric=mortality -->
- Cost: Brown rice, oats, and whole-wheat flour cost a bit more than white; no time. The hard part is adapting to the texture.
- In plain terms: People eating 90 g more whole grains a day run about 17% lower mortality over the same period. 90 g is about 3 servings. Up to about 200 g a day, it keeps dropping.
- Benefit: Pooled cohort studies (record-only): each extra 90 g of whole grains daily lowers mortality about 17% (RR 0.83). 90 g is about 3 servings. The benefit continues to 210–225 g daily. Another pool: each extra daily serving, about 8% lower (RR 0.92).
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Aune D et al. (2016). Whole grain consumption and risk of cardiovascular disease, cancer, and all cause and cause specific mortality: systematic review and dose-response meta-analysis of prospective studies. BMJ. <https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.i2716>；Schwingshackl L et al. (2017). Food groups and risk of all-cause mortality: a systematic review and meta-analysis of prospective studies. American Journal of Clinical Nutrition. <https://doi.org/10.3945/ajcn.117.153148>
- Note: No need to go all whole-grain — half gets you most of the visible benefit. Remember these studies only track; whole-grain eaters are healthier to begin with, so numbers run large. And the studies disagree widely (I² 83% — the higher, the less consistent).

### 25. Drink tea three or more times a week
<!-- costs: money=low time=low will=no benefit=medium metric=mortality -->
- Cost: A few dozen to a few hundred yuan a year. Brewing a cup takes minutes.
- In plain terms: Habitual tea drinkers run about 15% lower mortality over the same period. From age 50, that's 1.26 extra years of life on average, and 1.41 extra years free of cardiovascular disease.
- Benefit: A Chinese study of 100,900 people (China-PAR), half followed a full 7.3 years. Habitual tea drinkers ran about 15% lower mortality (hazard ratio 0.85, 95% CI 0.79–0.90, the confidence range). From age 50: tea drinkers got 1.41 more years free of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease and 1.26 more years of life expectancy
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Wang X, Liu F, Li J, et al. (2020). Tea consumption and the risk of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease and all-cause mortality: The China-PAR project. European Journal of Preventive Cardiology, 27(18), 1956-1963. <https://doi.org/10.1177/2047487319894685>；茶叶霉菌毒素：Cui P et al. (2020). Quantitative analysis and dietary risk assessment of aflatoxins in Chinese post-fermented dark tea. Food and Chemical Toxicology. <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.fct.2020.111830>；Zhou H et al. (2022). Mycotoxins in Tea (Camellia sinensis (L.) Kuntze): Contamination and Dietary Exposure Profiling in the Chinese Population. Toxins. <https://doi.org/10.3390/toxins14070452>；绿茶提取物与肝损伤：Hu J et al. (2018). The safety of green tea and green tea extract consumption in adults - Results of a systematic review. Regulatory Toxicology and Pharmacology. <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.yrtph.2018.03.019>
- Note: Contested: these studies only track, and Chinese male tea drinkers smoke and drink more; the authors adjusted for some of it, but not cleanly away. Also, don't drink it scalding — for hot-drink temperature, see this section's entry on it. On mycotoxin worries: of 158 domestic dark-tea samples checked, 2 had detectable aflatoxin, with intake below international acceptable levels at actual drinking amounts. Another survey of 352 samples across 16 mycotoxins found only ochratoxin A in dark tea above limits on average. At Chinese consumption levels, neither computed a dietary risk. Store tea dry; discard damp or moldy leaves. Green tea extract capsules are a different matter: concentrated catechins swallowed in bulk can injure the liver — brewed tea doesn't do this. So this entry means brewed tea only; don't substitute extract supplements

### 26. Drink three to four cups of coffee a day, no sugar, no cream
<!-- costs: money=low time=low will=no benefit=large metric=mortality -->
- Cost: Brewing your own runs 1–2 yuan a day. A cup takes minutes.
- In plain terms: Three to four cups a day runs about 17% lower mortality than none over the same period.
- Benefit: An umbrella review pooled 201 meta-analyses, whose underlying studies were all record-only. Result: 3–4 cups daily versus none, mortality 17% lower (relative risk 0.83, 95% CI 0.79–0.88, the confidence range)
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Poole R, Kennedy OJ, Roderick P, et al. (2017). Coffee consumption and health: umbrella review of meta-analyses of multiple health outcomes. BMJ, 359, j5024. <https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.j5024>
- Note: Contested: the authors state the evidence is almost entirely observational, and that "robust randomised controlled trials" would be needed to judge causation — tracking alone can't establish it. Drink it black; sugar and cream toppings cancel the benefit. Pregnant women, and people with arrhythmia, anxiety, or insomnia, skip this entry

### 27. Eat 5 servings (about 400 g) of fruit and vegetables a day
<!-- costs: money=low time=medium will=some benefit=medium metric=mortality -->
- Cost: A few to a dozen-odd yuan a day. Washing and cutting take some time daily. The hard part is buying and eating enough every single day.
- In plain terms: Each extra 200 g of fruit and vegetables daily lowers mortality about 10% over the same period. Five servings a day runs about 13% lower than two. Beyond that, no further drop.
- Benefit: Pooled cohort studies (record-only): each extra 200 g daily, mortality about 10% lower (RR 0.90). The benefit continues to 800 g a day. Two large US cohorts plus a 26-study pool: 5 servings a day ran about 13% lower mortality than 2 (HR 0.87). The best mix was 2 servings of fruit plus 3 of vegetables; more added nothing.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Aune D et al. (2017). Fruit and vegetable intake and the risk of cardiovascular disease, total cancer and all-cause mortality: a systematic review and dose-response meta-analysis of prospective studies. International Journal of Epidemiology. <https://doi.org/10.1093/ije/dyw319>；Wang DD et al. (2021). Fruit and Vegetable Intake and Mortality: Results From 2 Prospective Cohort Studies of US Men and Women and a Meta-Analysis of 26 Cohort Studies. Circulation. <https://doi.org/10.1161/CIRCULATIONAHA.120.048996>
- Note: 5 servings is enough — no need to push 10; both studies agree on that. Remember these studies only track, with heavy confounding: vegetable eaters differ in income, education, and exercise. So "about 10% lower" is already the ceiling.

### 28. Eat fewer ultra-processed foods (chips, instant noodles, pastries, ready meals)
<!-- costs: money=none time=high will=yes benefit=large metric=mortality -->
- Cost: Free — just buy differently. It means cooking yourself, or taking time to pick minimally processed ingredients, every day. The hard part is fighting the inertia of "convenient is fine" long-term.
- In plain terms: The heaviest ultra-processed-food eaters run about 20% higher mortality than the lightest over the same period, and 50% higher cardiovascular death. But this evidence is rated uncertain — discount the numbers accordingly.
- Benefit: An umbrella review pooled multiple meta-analyses, underlying studies all record-only. High versus low ultra-processed intake: mortality about 21% higher (RR 1.21), cardiovascular death about 50% higher (RR 1.50). These two carried evidence grades of "highly suggestive" and "convincing." But under the standard GRADE scoring, certainty was low or very low.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Lane MM et al. (2024). Ultra-processed food exposure and adverse health outcomes: umbrella review of epidemiological meta-analyses. BMJ. <https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj-2023-077310>
- Note: Contested on three counts. First, the NOVA classification lumps nutritionally very different foods together. Second, it overlaps heavily with sugary drinks and processed meat (items 7, 18) — you can't tell which is doing the work. Third, GRADE rates it low. So after doing those two, how much this one adds is unclear. The counter-evidence is the same review's low rating itself; no primary study reaches the opposite conclusion. Do items 7 and 18 first, then this one.

### 29. Cook and heat with electricity or gas, not coal or wood
<!-- costs: money=high time=low will=no benefit=large metric=mortality -->
- Cost: Rural households need new stoves plus fuel — a few hundred to over a thousand yuan a year. A one-time change. Urban dwellers are mostly there already.
- In plain terms: People burning coal or wood for cooking run about 10% higher mortality over the same period; for heating, about 14%. Those who've switched to electricity or gas run 13%–33% lower than those still burning solid fuel.
- Benefit: A Chinese chronic-disease cohort, record-only, 271,000 adults without cardiovascular disease. Solid-fuel cooks ran about 11% higher mortality than electricity-or-gas users (HR 1.11); solid-fuel heating, about 14% higher (HR 1.14). Those who had switched from solid to clean fuel, versus still burning: cooking about 13% lower (HR 0.87), heating about 33% lower (HR 0.67). Outdoor PM2.5 is a separate account: pooling 104 cohort studies, each 10 µg/m³ of long-term exposure raises natural-cause mortality about 8% (RR 1.08). Natural cause means non-accidental death from illness.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Yu K et al. (2018). Association of Solid Fuel Use With Risk of Cardiovascular and All-Cause Mortality in Rural China. JAMA. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2018.2151>；Chen J, Hoek G (2020). Long-term exposure to PM and all-cause and cause-specific mortality: A systematic review and meta-analysis. Environment International. <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.envint.2020.105974>
- Note: These studies only track. Families that switched fuel are also wealthier, so "risk down to 0.67×" contains some household-income effect. As for outdoor PM2.5, personal options are limited — moving, masks, purifiers. Air purifiers have no mortality-endpoint studies, so this section quotes no numbers.

### 30. Let hot things cool a bit — no scalding tea, soup, or coffee
<!-- costs: money=none time=low will=some benefit=large metric=mortality -->
- Cost: Free; two or three extra minutes each time. The hard part is holding back when it smells good.
- In plain terms: Drinking very hot tea carries 8 times the esophageal cancer risk of warm tea. Even "hot" doubles it. Drinking within two minutes of pouring runs 5 times the risk of waiting four minutes or more.
- Benefit: In a high-incidence region of northern Iran, 300 patients were compared with 571 without the disease. All versus warm-tea drinkers: "hot" tea carried about 2.07 times the odds of esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (odds ratio, 95% CI 1.28–3.35, the confidence range); "very hot," about 8.16 times (3.93–16.9). Drinking within 2 minutes of pouring ran about 5.41 times the odds of waiting 4+ minutes (2.63–11.1)
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Islami F, Pourshams A, Nasrollahzadeh D, et al. (2009). Tea drinking habits and oesophageal cancer in a high risk area in northern Iran: population based case-control study. BMJ, 338, b929. <https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.b929>；Loomis D, Guyton KZ, Grosse Y, et al. (2016). Carcinogenicity of drinking coffee, mate, and very hot beverages. Lancet Oncology, 17(7), 877-878. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S1470-2045(16)30239-X>
- Note: The IARC classifies beverages above 65 °C as Group 2A — probably carcinogenic. The same assessment cleared coffee itself. China's Chaoshan and Taihang regions have high esophageal cancer rates, tied to the drink-it-scalding habit

### 31. Get daylight when you're out by day — don't live unlit
<!-- costs: money=none time=low will=no benefit=medium metric=mortality -->
- Cost: Free. A dozen-odd minutes a day; commutes and lunch breaks cover it.
- In plain terms: Women who deliberately avoid the sun lose 0.6–2.1 years of life expectancy versus the most sun-exposed. Non-smokers who avoid the sun live about as long as the heaviest smokers who bask.
- Benefit: A Swedish study, 29,500 women, 20 years of follow-up. Versus the most-exposed group, deliberate sun-avoiders lost 0.6–2.1 years of life expectancy. The authors wrote that "the avoidance of sun exposure was a risk factor for death of a similar magnitude as smoking"
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Lindqvist PG, Epstein E, Nielsen K, et al. (2016). Avoidance of sun exposure as a risk factor for major causes of death: a competing risk analysis of the Melanoma in Southern Sweden cohort. Journal of Internal Medicine, 280(4), 375-387. <https://doi.org/10.1111/joim.12496>
- Note: Contested: Sweden sits at high latitude with weak sun, so the finding doesn't transfer cleanly to China. Sun-avoiders may also be frailer and more housebound to begin with. Don't burn either — cover up at midday, since sun exposure raises skin cancer risk

### 32. Keep BMI at 20–25; if overweight, lose it
<!-- costs: money=none time=high will=yes benefit=large metric=mortality -->
- Cost: Free. Diet and exercise take time daily. This is the section's most willpower-hungry entry: managing intake and moving for the long haul — and keeping it off is harder than losing it.
- In plain terms: Mortality is lowest in the BMI 20–25 band. At 27.5–30 it's about 20% higher; 30–35, about 45%; 35–40, nearly double. East Asians are more sensitive to excess weight: every 5 points of BMI adds about 40% risk.
- Benefit: Individual data pooled from 239 prospective studies (record-only). The analysis kept only three kinds of people: never-smokers, those free of chronic disease at enrollment, and those who survived 5+ years after enrollment. Result: mortality lowest at BMI 20–25. BMI 25–27.5: about 7% higher (HR 1.07). 27.5–30: about 20% (1.20). 30–35: about 45% (1.45). 35–40: about 94% (1.94). 40–60: 2.76 times (2.76). Above BMI 25, each extra 5 kg/m² added about 39% for East Asians (HR 1.39).
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Global BMI Mortality Collaboration (2016). Body-mass index and all-cause mortality: individual-participant-data meta-analysis of 239 prospective studies in four continents. Lancet. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(16)30175-1>；Flegal KM et al. (2013). Association of all-cause mortality with overweight and obesity using standard body mass index categories: a systematic review and meta-analysis. JAMA. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2012.113905>（争议方）
- Note: Contested. One camp holds that "a bit overweight actually prolongs life." Their pool found overweight about 6% lower mortality, mild obesity about 5% lower. The disagreement is mostly over whether to drop three kinds of data: smokers, the already-ill, and the first years after enrollment. They're dropped because the gravely ill lose weight first. Once the 239-study analysis dropped all three, the overweight risk reappeared. Also note: no randomized trial proves that losing weight itself lowers all-cause mortality. The benefit above is a cross-sectional comparison of differently-sized people — not a promise that slimming cuts your risk by that much. If you're losing weight, don't bother with meal timing: breakfast and 16:8 fasting add nothing — see Section 6, item 26 (don't count on breakfast or 16:8 for weight control)
### 33. Eat chili four or more times a week
<!-- costs: money=none time=low will=no benefit=large metric=mortality -->
- Cost: Free, no time.
- In plain terms: People eating chili 4+ times a week run about 23% lower mortality than rare eaters over the same period, and about a third lower cardiovascular death.
- Benefit: An Italian study of 22,800 people, half followed a full 8.2 years, 1,236 deaths. Chili 4+ times weekly versus rarely: mortality about 23% lower (hazard ratio 0.77, 95% CI 0.66–0.90, the confidence range). Cardiovascular death about 34% lower (0.66, 0.50–0.86)
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Bonaccio M, Di Castelnuovo A, Costanzo S, et al. (2019). Chili Pepper Consumption and Mortality in Italian Adults. Journal of the American College of Cardiology, 74(25), 3139-3149. <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jacc.2019.09.068>
- Note: Contested: this is record-keeping against a Mediterranean-diet backdrop, and chili eaters may simply eat closer to the local tradition. Also, people with reflux, hemorrhoids, or irritable bowel get worse symptoms from chili — don't force it

### 34. Drink one or two servings of milk or yogurt a day
<!-- costs: money=low time=low will=no benefit=large metric=mortality -->
- Cost: A few yuan a day, no time.
- In plain terms: People having two or more daily servings of milk or yogurt run about 17% lower mortality than non-consumers over the same period.
- Benefit: The PURE cohort spans 21 countries, 136,000 people, average 9.1 years, 6,796 deaths. Two-plus daily dairy servings versus none: mortality about 17% lower (hazard ratio 0.83, 95% CI 0.72–0.96, the confidence range), trend P=0.0052
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Dehghan M, Mente A, Rangarajan S, et al. (2018). Association of dairy intake with cardiovascular disease and mortality in 21 countries from five continents (PURE): a prospective cohort study. Lancet, 392(10161), 2288-2297. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(18)31812-9>
- Note: Contested: PURE includes many low- and middle-income countries where being able to afford daily dairy itself signals means, and that confound resists cleaning. The lactose-intolerant can switch to yogurt or low-lactose milk — no need to force cow's milk

### 35. Eggs needn't be banned — just not three or four a day
<!-- costs: money=none time=low will=no benefit=small metric=mortality -->
- Cost: Free, no time.
- In plain terms: Each extra half egg a day adds about 7% mortality over the same period. Swap that half egg for egg whites, fish, poultry, or nuts, and every cause-of-death risk comes out lower. No need to quit eggs — just don't eat three or four daily.
- Benefit: A US cohort of 521,000 people, 129,000 deaths. Each extra half whole egg daily: mortality about 7% higher (hazard ratio 1.07, 95% CI 1.06–1.08, the confidence range). Swapping that half egg for equal amounts of egg whites, poultry, fish, dairy, or nuts and legumes meant lower mortality — cardiovascular, cancer, and respiratory deaths all lower too
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Zhuang P, Wu F, Mao L, et al. (2021). Egg and cholesterol consumption and mortality from cardiovascular and different causes in the United States: A population-based cohort study. PLoS Medicine, 18(2), e1003508. <https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pmed.1003508>
- Note: Heavily contested: other large cohorts and pools found no egg–mortality link. The US dietary guidelines dropped the daily cholesterol cap. What to avoid is three or four a day; one a day is nothing to worry about

### 36. Bathe when you can, don't just shower
<!-- costs: money=low time=medium will=no benefit=large metric=mortality -->
- Cost: More water and electricity. Half an hour a soak.
- In plain terms: Near-daily bathers run about 28% lower cardiovascular events than those bathing under twice a week, and about 46% lower brain hemorrhage.
- Benefit: A Japanese study of 30,000 people, 19 years, 2,097 cardiovascular events. Near-daily bathing versus at most twice weekly: cardiovascular events about 28% lower (hazard ratio 0.72, 95% CI 0.62–0.84, the confidence range). Coronary heart disease about 35% lower (0.65, 0.45–0.94). Stroke about 26% (0.74, 0.62–0.87). Brain hemorrhage about 46% (0.54, 0.40–0.73)
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Ukai T, Iso H, Yamagishi K, et al. (2020). Habitual tub bathing and risks of incident coronary heart disease and stroke. Heart, 106(10), 732-737. <https://doi.org/10.1136/heartjnl-2019-315752>
- Note: Contested: this comes from a Japanese population and bathtub culture; most Chinese households can't soak daily. Don't overheat the water or soak too long — for the elderly and those with cardiovascular disease, very hot water and long soaks are dangerous, and Japan records many elderly bathtub deaths yearly

### 37. Keep naps under half an hour, never past an hour; if you truly need 1–2 hours to function, get checked
<!-- costs: money=none time=low will=no benefit=medium metric=mortality -->
- Cost: Free. Set an alarm before dozing off; almost no willpower needed.
- In plain terms: Napping under an hour raises neither mortality nor chronic-disease risk, and the mind is sharper. Past an hour: coronary risk up about 30%, diabetes and obesity up about 20%. So set an alarm for half an hour. And if you truly need 1–2 hours daily to function, that's a signal to get examined.
- Benefit: An umbrella review pooled 16 meta-analyses, 244 health outcomes. An umbrella review re-pools existing pools. Conclusion: naps under 60 minutes raised neither total mortality nor chronic-disease risk, with the clearest cognitive improvement (SMD 0.69, 95% CI 0.37–1.00, the confidence range). Short naps of 20–30 minutes best improved physical performance (SMD 0.99, 0.67–1.31). Naps over 60 minutes: coronary risk about 30% higher, diabetes and obesity about 20% higher. Another study had 1,338 people over 56 wear wristwatches to measure naps directly rather than self-report. Follow-up up to 19 years, 926 deaths. Each extra hour of napping: mortality about 13% higher (HR 1.13, 95% CI 1.04–1.23). Each extra daily nap: about 7% higher (HR 1.07, 1.02–1.13). Morning nappers ran about 30% higher than early-afternoon nappers (HR 1.30, 1.03–1.64).
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Du P, Li J, Hua Z, et al. (2026). Multiple Health Outcomes of Daytime Napping: A Comprehensive Umbrella Review. Public Health Reviews. <https://doi.org/10.3389/phrs.2026.1609013>；Gao C, Cai R, Zheng X, et al. (2026). Objectively Measured Daytime Napping Patterns and All-Cause Mortality in Older Adults. JAMA Network Open. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2026.7938>；Dashti HS et al. (2021). Genetic determinants of daytime napping and effects on cardiometabolic health. Nature Communications. <https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-020-20585-3>
- Note: Contested. Both studies are record-only. Long nappers are likelier to have sleep apnea, anemia, hypothyroidism, depression, or other chronic illness. So how much of "long nappers die more" the napping itself causes is unanswerable. Genetic causal inference (Mendelian randomization, 453,000 plus 541,000 people) supports exactly one thing: more frequent napping goes with slightly higher blood pressure and waistline. It found no causal effect of napping on coronary disease or diabetes. So don't treat "nap less" as a mortality reducer. The "get checked" half is author advice, Grade C: if daytime sleepiness forces long naps, check night sleep first — snoring and breathing stops mean a sleep-apnea workup — then blood counts and thyroid. For how a short nap refreshes: Section 3, item 11 (10 minutes when the afternoon slump hits). For night duration: item 13 here.

### 38. After a late night, catch up the very next night — don't bank it for the weekend
<!-- costs: money=none time=low will=no benefit=medium metric=mortality -->
- Cost: Free. The catch-up hour or two is sleep you wanted anyway.
- In plain terms: People who short their sleep and don't make it up the next night run about 15% higher mortality than the regular-scheduled; severely short without catch-up, about 42% higher. Those who caught up showed no elevated risk. So after a late night, go to bed early the next night.
- Benefit: UK Biobank, 85,618 people, average age 61.8, wristwatch-recorded daily sleep, median 8 years. The study computed "short-sleep nights" per person — 2.5+ hours below one's own need. The first night after a short night counted as the "rebound night," qualifying as caught-up if it exceeded one's need. Five patterns emerged. Versus regular sleepers: short-without-catch-up ran about 15% higher all-cause mortality (HR 1.15, 95% CI 1.01–1.31, the confidence range); cumulative shortfall over 3.5 hours without catch-up, about 42% higher (HR 1.42, 1.24–1.63). Both caught-up groups showed no significant association — short-then-caught-up was HR 1.12 (0.98–1.28). Among habitually short sleepers, short-without-catch-up ran about 19% higher (1.19, 1.01–1.40), severe-without-catch-up about 38% (1.38, 1.17–1.63). The result replicated in a US survey cohort of 4,586.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Li X, Zhang M, Li Z, et al. (2026). Acute sleep rebound following sleep restriction is associated with reduced mortality risk. Nature Communications. <https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-026-72461-1>
- Note: This is record-only. Those who can't catch up may be busier and sicker to begin with — not fully cleanable. "Caught-up groups showed no elevated risk" means no difference was detected; their point estimates still exceed 1, so catching up isn't a license. Repay late nights promptly, and don't treat that as permission to burn them freely. Catch-up here means the first night after a short one — not banking it for the weekend. Making "short weekdays, weekend catch-up" a weekly rhythm is social jet lag, itself tied to cardiovascular disease — see item 13 (about 7 hours, fixed schedule). The fixed-weekend-waketime entry is Section 3, item 2 — no conflict: this one says go to bed early the next night, not sleep till noon.

### 39. The longer you work night shifts, the higher the cardiovascular risk — transfer out as early as you can
<!-- costs: money=high time=medium will=yes benefit=large metric=mortality -->
- Cost: Transferring or changing jobs may cut pay and lose the shift allowance. Job-hunting takes time and resolve. If you're choosing a job and price this in up front, the cost is zero.
- In plain terms: Night-shift workers have about 13% more cardiovascular disease than non-shift, and about 27% more cardiovascular death. It scales with years: every 5 more years of nights adds roughly 7% more disease. So this account is settled in years — transfer early if you can. As for shift work causing cancer, the evidence is far softer than the lore.
- Benefit: Pooling 23 cohort studies, record-only. Night-shift workers: cardiovascular events about 13% higher (RR 1.13, 95% CI 1.10–1.16, the confidence range). Cardiovascular death about 27% higher (RR 1.27, 1.18–1.36). By years: every 5 more years of night shifts added about 7% more cardiovascular disease (RR 1.07, 1.04–1.09) and about 5% more cardiovascular death (RR 1.05, 1.03–1.06). By disease: coronary incidence about 22% higher (1.22, 1.16–1.28), coronary death about 22% (1.22, 1.10–1.36), ischemic heart disease death about 39% (1.39, 1.06–1.84), stroke death about 49% (1.49, 1.04–2.12). Stroke incidence was nonsignificant (1.06, 0.95–1.18).
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Xi J, Ma W, Tao Y, et al. (2025). Association between night shift work and cardiovascular disease: a systematic review and dose-response meta-analysis. Frontiers in Public Health. <https://doi.org/10.3389/fpubh.2025.1668848>；Esposito G, Bravi F, Santucci C, et al. (2025). Night shift work and breast cancer risk in healthcare workers: a systematic review and meta-analysis. Occupational Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.1093/occmed/kqaf040>；Shen QM, Li ZY, Tan YT, et al. (2026). Night shift work and risk of total and site-specific cancer: results from a prospective cohort study among Chinese men. Scandinavian Journal of Work, Environment & Health. <https://doi.org/10.5271/sjweh.4290>；Czeisler CA, Johnson MP, Duffy JF, et al. (1990). Exposure to bright light and darkness to treat physiologic maladaptation to night work. New England Journal of Medicine, 322(18), 1253-1259. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJM199005033221801>
- Note: All record-only. Night workers differ in smoking, weight, and income, never fully adjusted away. On night shifts causing cancer, the evidence is much softer than the lore: in a pool of 12 studies, 12,132 breast cancers, ever-shift workers ran just 5% higher with a confidence range crossing 1 (RR 1.05, 0.96–1.14). The 20-year-plus band ran 25% higher (1.25, 1.01–1.55), but that lost significance under influence analysis and neared 1 after publication-bias adjustment — the authors called the association "far from established." A Chinese cohort of men followed 16.1 years, 8,202 cancers, found no link between night shifts and total or most common cancers; only pancreatic cancer in the 11–20 cumulative-year band ran about 59% higher (HR 1.59, 1.09–2.31). So this entry settles accounts on cardiovascular grounds, not cancer. Not everyone should quit nights — the year-count account is yours to run. If you're staying, fix what's fixable: smoking, item 1; blood pressure and lipids, item 12; sleep recovery after shifts, item 38. Two tactics to try: concentrate eating into daytime, and shift the body clock with bright light plus strict daytime darkness — the only method proven to actually move the clock. Under ordinary indoor lighting, six consecutive night shifts leave the clock unmoved; with 7,000–12,000 lux bright light plus near-total daytime darkness, it inverts fully in four days. **Bright enough light at night, strict blackout for day sleep, and sunglasses against morning light on the way home — all three, or it doesn't work**; skip the last one and it's wasted. Both tactics' endpoints reach only metabolic markers and sleep; no trial measured whether they cut cardiovascular risk. Living days again on rest days pushes the clock back. Doses, original numbers, further sources, and how the body tells time are in [docs/生物钟和夜班.md](../docs/生物钟和夜班.md).

### 40. Buy prepackaged cooking oil with an SC number — not loose workshop-pressed peanut oil
<!-- costs: money=none time=low will=no benefit=medium metric=mortality -->
- Cost: Same money, different shelf. Check the pack for an SC-prefixed food-production license number. The hard part is persuading elders who always buy home-pressed oil.
- In plain terms: Damp, moldy peanuts grow aflatoxin, a definite carcinogen that hits the liver hardest. After Guangzhou tightened workshop rules, residents who often ate home-pressed oil had about 35% fewer abnormal liver-function readings. Guangxi mothers-to-be eating home-pressed peanut oil ran about 90% higher risk of low birth weight and about 80% higher preterm birth.
- Benefit: Aflatoxin is an IARC Group 1 carcinogen (definitely causes human cancer), mainly liver. A Guangzhou study measured aflatoxin B1 in workshop-pressed peanut oil, 2010–2022. Median 1.29 μg/kg, middle half of samples between 0.12 and 6.58. After local workshop regulations took effect, toxin levels immediately dropped 2.865 μg/kg, then another 2.593 yearly. Abnormal liver function among residents of the high-consumption area fell accordingly (PR 0.650, 95% CI 0.469–0.902, the confidence range) — about 35% lower. A Guangxi cohort of 1,611 pregnant women: 81.7% ate home-pressed peanut oil. Their babies ran about 90% higher risk of low birth weight (aOR 1.9, 1.1–3.2) and about 80% higher preterm birth (aOR 1.8, 1.1–3.0).
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Lei J, Li Y, Wang Y, et al. (2024). The impact of small food workshops management regulations on aflatoxin B1 in home-made peanut oil and the liver function of high-consumption area residents: an interrupted time series study in Guangzhou, China. ### 41. Cook with vegetable oil instead of lard and butter; don't shop oils for health, and don't expect flaxseed oil to protect your heart
<!-- costs: money=none time=low will=some benefit=medium metric=mortality -->
- Cost: No extra money. Vegetable oil is most households' default anyway. The hard part is giving up the lard stir-fry taste.
- In plain terms: Eating less saturated fat — lard, butter — and swapping in vegetable oil cuts heart attacks and strokes by about 17%, but total mortality doesn't budge. Soybean, corn, and canola oils are interchangeable — no evidence separates them. Flaxseed oil in quantity shows no drop in heart disease either.
- Benefit: Cochrane pooled 15 randomized trials, 56,675 people, each running at least 2 years. Less saturated fat: combined cardiovascular events about 17% lower (RR 0.83, 0.70–0.98). Total mortality essentially unchanged (RR 0.96, 0.90–1.03), cardiovascular death too (RR 0.95, 0.80–1.12). Replacing with polyunsaturated fat (the bulk of soybean and corn oil) versus carbohydrates: no detectable difference. Monounsaturated (canola, olive): too little data. Another Cochrane pool, 19 trials, 6,461 people: extra omega-6 (linoleic acid) — total mortality RR 1.00 (0.88–1.12), cardiovascular events RR 0.97 (0.81–1.15): no benefit, no harm. A third pool, 86 trials, 162,796 people: extra plant omega-3 (flaxseed and perilla oil) — total mortality RR 1.01 (0.84–1.20), coronary events RR 1.00 (0.82–1.22): essentially nothing.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Hooper L, Martin N, Jimoh OF, et al. (2020). Reduction in saturated fat for cardiovascular disease. Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, (5), CD011737. <https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD011737.pub3>；Hooper L, Al-Khudairy L, Abdelhamid AS, et al. (2018). Omega-6 fats for the primary and secondary prevention of cardiovascular disease. Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, (11), CD011094. <https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD011094.pub4>；Abdelhamid AS, Brown TJ, Brainard JS, et al. (2020). Omega-3 fatty acids for the primary and secondary prevention of cardiovascular disease. Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, (3), CD003177. <https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD003177.pub5>；中国营养学会 (2022). 中国居民膳食指南（2022）. 人民卫生出版社
- Note: Contested: some argue the omega-6 in soybean and corn oil is pro-inflammatory, so eat less or switch. The omega-6 randomized trials above show no harm, at self-rated low quality. The benefit is sized "medium" on the 17% event reduction; total mortality didn't move. Whatever the oil, control the amount — the dietary guideline says 25–30 g of cooking oil per person per day.

### 42. Run the range hood from the moment you fire up until you finish cooking or frying
<!-- costs: money=none time=low will=some benefit=large metric=mortality -->
- Cost: If you have a hood, a little electricity. If not, buying and installing one runs a few hundred to over a thousand yuan. The hard part is turning it on every single time and not switching it off because it's loud.
- In plain terms: Non-smoking Chinese women who rarely or never run the hood while stir-frying run about double the lung cancer risk of habitual users. Living in a home with a poorly ventilated kitchen adds about 50%, most visibly in coal-burning households.
- Benefit: Pooling 10 case-control studies of non-smoking Chinese women. Case-control compares patients with the healthy and asks about past habits. Cooking-oil-fume exposure carried about 1.74 times the odds of lung cancer (odds ratio, 95% CI 1.57–1.94, the confidence range). The 4 studies isolating fume exposure without a hood: about 2.11 times (1.54–2.89). A Taiwanese study matched 1,302 non-smoking female lung cancer patients with 1,302 healthy women, restricted to 10+ years of cooking. Hood use in over two-thirds of those years versus under a third: risk about halved (odds ratio 0.49, 0.32–0.76). A Shanghai cohort followed 71,000 non-smoking women from 1996 to 2009, finding 429 lung cancers. Self-reported living in poorly ventilated kitchens: about 49% higher risk (HR 1.49, 1.15–1.95). Coal plus poor ventilation for 20+ years: about 2.03 times (HR 2.03, 1.35–3.05). Coal alone showed no difference (HR 1.03, 0.84–1.26). Cooking-oil type: no difference either.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Xue Y, Jiang Y, Jin S, Li Y (2016). Association between cooking oil fume exposure and lung cancer among Chinese nonsmoking women: a meta-analysis. OncoTargets and Therapy, 9, 2987-2992. <https://doi.org/10.2147/OTT.S100949>；Chen TY, Fang YH, Chen HL, et al. (2020). Impact of cooking oil fume exposure and fume extractor use on lung cancer risk in non-smoking Han Chinese women. Scientific Reports, 10, 6774. <https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-020-63656-7>；Kim C, Gao YT, Xiang YB, et al. (2015). Home kitchen ventilation, cooking fuels, and lung cancer risk in a prospective cohort of never smoking women in Shanghai, China. International Journal of Cancer, 136(3), 632-638. <https://doi.org/10.1002/ijc.29020>
- Note: Contested: the pro-hood numbers come mostly from case-control studies. Patients recalling the past after diagnosis tend to under-report hood use, inflating the numbers. In the Shanghai cohort, the elevated risk concentrated in coal-plus-poor-ventilation homes; gas-only homes got no separate number. Hence only Grade B, with the benefit sized "large" on the roughly-twofold risk difference. For not burning coal or wood: item 29. The beneficiary is mainly whoever rules your stove.

### 43. If hot flashes and night sweats around menopause disrupt your life, get evaluated for hormone therapy — don't tough it out, don't self-medicate, and don't use it to prevent heart disease
<!-- costs: money=low time=medium will=no benefit=large metric=mortality -->
- Cost: One visit to gynecology or a menopause clinic. The doctor takes a history, checks for contraindications, and prescribes if appropriate. Follow-up as instructed. The hard part is letting go of the fear that "hormones mean cancer" — and not buying them for anti-aging.
- In plain terms: For menopausal hot flashes and night sweats, hormone therapy cuts hot-flash frequency by about 75% versus placebo. The price: slightly more breast cancer, clots, and stroke — about 8 extra cases each per 10,000 women per year. Starting at 50–59 showed no rise in total mortality. It is not for preventing heart disease or dementia.
- Benefit: Cochrane pooled 24 randomized trials, 3,329 people, oral hormones versus placebo. Hormone users had about 75% fewer weekly hot flashes than placebo (95% CI 64.3–82.3, the confidence range). Severity also fell sharply (OR 0.13, 0.07–0.23). Placebo users' hot flashes fell about 57.7% too — anything claiming to treat hot flashes must beat placebo to count. Risks come from the US WHI randomized trial: 16,608 women aged 50–79 with a uterus, estrogen plus progestin, average 5.2 years. Coronary disease about 29% higher (HR 1.29, 1.02–1.63). Invasive breast cancer about 26% (HR 1.26, 1.00–1.59). Stroke about 41% (HR 1.41, 1.07–1.85). Pulmonary embolism about 2.13 times (HR 2.13, 1.39–3.25). In absolute terms per 10,000 women per year: 7 more coronary events, 8 more strokes, 8 more pulmonary embolisms, 8 more invasive breast cancers — and 6 fewer colorectal cancers, 5 fewer hip fractures. Women without a uterus on estrogen alone, followed 13 years, ran about 21% lower breast cancer (HR 0.79, 0.65–0.97). Both trials pooled over 18 years: total mortality unchanged (HR 0.99, 0.94–1.03). By starting age, the 50–59 group ran about 31% lower mortality during treatment years (HR 0.69, 0.51–0.94); 60–69 was 1.04 (0.87–1.25) and 70–79 1.13 (0.94–1.36), both nonsignificant. At 18 years, the 50–59 group was 0.89 (0.79–1.01), also nonsignificant. Cochrane's 2017 review, women 50–59 only: the one clear increase was clots on combined therapy, absolute risk under 1/500. Women over 65 on combined therapy: dementia rose from 9 to 11–30 cases per thousand over 4 years. The US Preventive Services Task Force 2022 recommends against hormones for chronic-disease prevention (grade D) — no net benefit.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: MacLennan AH, Broadbent JL, Lester S, Moore V (2004). Oral oestrogen and combined oestrogen/progestogen therapy versus placebo for hot flushes. Cochrane also notes the trials lack power for women within 10 years of menopause. Hormones also reduce fractures, but are usually considered only when other osteoporosis drugs don't suit. For osteoporosis, see Section 1, items 39 (bone density) and 40 (medicate it). Benefit sized "large" on the 75% hot-flash reduction — a symptom endpoint, not mortality. The beneficiary is mainly you, and your mother and partner too.

### 44. If you already want a dog, count the daily walk toward your step total — don't get one for longevity
<!-- costs: money=high time=high will=yes benefit=large metric=mortality -->
- Cost: A dog is permanent spending — food, vaccines, deworming, vet visits, registration — until it dies of old age. Daily walking time, and someone to cover travel. The hard part is doing it every day; a dog isn't something you can stop wanting. No official Chinese statistics on dog costs, so no numbers here.
- In plain terms: Dog owners run about a quarter lower mortality on average over the same period, and lower still after heart attack or stroke. But after adjustment, the big UK and Norwegian studies see no difference — and cats show no association either. So don't get a dog for longevity; if you wanted one anyway, count the walks as your walking.
- Benefit: Pooling 10 cohort studies, about 3.84 million people, average 10.1 years. Dog owners: mortality about 24% lower (RR 0.76, 95% CI 0.67–0.86, the confidence range). Cardiovascular death about 31% lower (RR 0.69, 0.67–0.71). Among coronary patients, dog owners ran about 65% lower (RR 0.35, 0.17–0.69). The authors state this pool applied no adjustment. Sweden's registry of ~3.43 million adjusted for income, marriage, and more. Dog ownership for people living alone: mortality about 33% lower (HR 0.67, 0.65–0.69); living with others, about 11% (HR 0.89, 0.87–0.91). Post-heart-attack in Sweden: living alone with a dog, about 33% lower (HR 0.67, 0.61–0.75); with partner or child, about 15% (HR 0.85, 0.80–0.90). Post-ischemic-stroke: about 27% (HR 0.73, 0.66–0.80) and 12% (HR 0.88, 0.83–0.93). The other side: 6 UK survey waves, 59,352 people, average 11.5 years. Fully adjusted, dog ownership showed no association with death (HR 1.03, 0.98–1.09) or cardiovascular death (HR 1.07, 0.96–1.18). A Norwegian study, median 18.5 years: dog owners' mortality essentially identical (HR 1.00, 0.91–1.09), and their activity wasn't higher either. The Swedish twin registry, 34,202 people with lifestyle data, failed to reproduce the lower cardiovascular and mortality risk. An Australian study, 15,735 people over 4 years: dog owners about 23% lower mortality (OR 0.77, 0.59–0.99), partly explained by activity. Cats, birds, and fish show no mortality association. The American Heart Association's 2013 statement rated pet ownership, especially dogs, "may be reasonable" for reducing cardiovascular risk — the weakest tier — and explicitly opposed adopting, rescuing, or buying pets primarily for cardiovascular risk reduction.
